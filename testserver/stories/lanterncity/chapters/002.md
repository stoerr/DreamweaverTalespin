# The Door in the Tidepool

Where Mara returns to the shore, follows a trail of impossible clues, and discovers a hidden door in a tidepool that leads downward instead of in.

By morning, the storm had remembered it had other places to be.

The sky over the town was scrubbed clean and pale, streaked with wisps of cloud that looked like they’d been left behind by a distracted painter. The sea, having shouted itself hoarse all night, lay sprawled along the shore in a mood that might generously be called “apologetic,” ruffling the edge of the sand with small, guilty waves.

None of this helped Mara Bright’s concentration in the slightest.

Her cereal had gone from crisp to soggy in record time while she stared past it, seeing not the kitchen window with its view of the neighbour’s thoroughly uninteresting shed, but a dim museum hall, a dripping helmet, and a wall that had pulsed like a slow, enormous heart under her hand.

“Eat,” said her mother, without looking up from the tablet propped against the sugar jar. “You’ll be late.”

“I am eating,” Mara said automatically, though what she was doing could more accurately be described as prodding. “Technically.”

Her father lowered his newspaper just enough to peer at her over the edge. “You’re quiet,” he observed. “Even for you.”

“I’m thinking,” Mara replied, because that was easier than saying I touched a building and it thought back at me.

“About what?” her mother asked, still skimming headlines with the intensity of someone hoping the news might, just once, be about something pleasant like kittens winning the lottery.

Mara hesitated. “Maps,” she said finally. It wasn’t untrue. “And… museums.”

“The one on the front?” her father said, brightening. “Captain Something Kelp? They used to have a very stern octopus in a jar. I don’t know what happened to it.”

“Legislation, probably,” her mother murmured. “Or ethics.”

Mara chewed, swallowed, and found that “Captain Thimble Kelp” caught on something inside her. She turned the name over in her mind the way she might turn over a shell, checking for hidden patterns. The memory of his lined face, his storm-tossed hair, the way his fingers had tightened on her wrist—those clung like salt to skin.

“Are you going out after?” her mother asked. “You can, just—stay where there are people, all right? No wandering off down strange alleys or climbing derelict piers or befriending seals. They carry diseases.”

“You can’t get diseases from befriending seals,” Mara said.

“Emotional diseases,” her father put in. “Attachment. Separation anxiety. Fish breath.”

“Ha,” said her mother, entirely without humour. “Anyway, text if you change locations. Please. That’s all I ask.”

It was not all she asked. There was also Eat vegetables and Don’t lick strange things no matter what your cousin dares you, and the evergreen favourite Don’t go swimming alone. But she didn’t say those, because she trusted repetition to have carved them into Mara’s brain already.

“I’ll stay on the beach,” Mara said. Which again, was not exactly a lie. The beach was, after all, technically people-adjacent. “Tide’s going out. It’s nice.”

Her father made a thoughtful noise. “Watch out for jellyfish,” he said. “Those pink ones have an agenda.”

Mara finished the last of the now thoroughly unpleasant cereal, grabbed her jacket, and slung her backpack over one shoulder.

On her way to the door, her mother called, “Mara? Phone!”

Mara patted her pockets and came up with the small, scratched rectangle. “Got it!”

“On,” her mother added. “And not on silent.”

“You’re ruining my mysterious loner aesthetic,” Mara muttered, but she flicked the volume up anyway. It gave a small, startled chirp, as if it hadn’t been allowed to speak in days.

Outside, the air tasted like rinsed glass. The world had that particular brightness it only ever had after storms, when every surface was freshly washed and the colours hadn’t had time to fade back to normal levels of disappointment. Puddles still collected in the dips of the pavement, reflecting slices of sky. A gull strutted along the railings in the firm belief that it owned all horizons.

Mara’s feet knew the way even without her thinking about it: down the road that sloped toward the sea, past the cluster of souvenir shops still shuttered, their postcards trembling in their racks; across the car park that always smelled faintly of fried onions no matter the time of day; and onto the promenade, where the sea spread out before her, blue and deceptively simple.

The museum crouched in its familiar place between the fish-and-chips shop and the dead arcade, its flaking green door and KELP & CURIOSITIES sign looking no more or less improbable than the day before. If she squinted, Mara could believe that the walls bulged slightly, as though holding in more space than their dimensions should allow.

She slowed as she approached, half expecting the doorknob to twitch, the CLOSED-ISH sign to flick over to OPEN of its own accord. It didn’t. The windows were dull and faintly reflective. A hand-painted note had been wedged against the glass, scrawled in a hasty, spidery script.

BACK IN A BIT  
TIDE BUSINESS  
—C.T.K.

“Tide business?” she murmured. “Is that like monkey business, but wetter?”

The note stared back, unhelpful.

Mara felt an immediate, sharp pinch of disappointment. Her plan—such as it was—had been to march in, ask Captain Thimble Kelp as many questions as he could withstand, and hope that somewhere between “accidental” and “annoying,” some answers would leak out.

Instead, the museum was stubbornly, smugly shut.

She turned away, toward the sea.

The tide had retreated considerably since dawn, dragging the waterline back to reveal a long stretch of wet sand and, beyond that, the darker, jagged scatter of rocks that made up the lower beach. Normally, she avoided that part; it was full of treacherous algae and sharp edges and the sort of crannies that invited ankles to twist.

Today, the exposed rocks gleamed like the slick backs of strange, sleeping animals. Seawater puddled in hollows, catching the sunlight. The hiss and susurrus of the waves was broken up now by the smaller sounds of the revealed shore: trickles, drips, the occasional plop of something dropping back into somewhere.

Mara hopped down the concrete steps from the promenade to the sand. Her shoes sank slightly; the sand was firm but damp, a cool give under her soles. The air here smelled more strongly of salt and less of chips. Gulls wheeled overhead, arguing.

She pulled in a lungful of sea air. It felt… different.

Not bad. Just… crowded. As if she’d stepped into a room mid-conversation and everyone had gone silent in exactly the way that meant they were still talking, just at a pitch she couldn’t hear.

The waves, sliding in and out over the sand, hissed like someone whispering secrets through clenched teeth.

Mara frowned, tilting her head, the way you do when you’re sure you heard your name and then realise you didn’t. The sound of the water seemed almost patterned. Not quite speech—but not quite random, either.

“You’re being weird,” she informed the sea. “Again.”

A small, entirely ordinary wavelets lapped at her toes in what might have been an indifferent shrug.

Fine. If the museum was closed and the sea was being cryptic, she would do what she always did when adults were unhelpful: look around more carefully.

Her gaze drifted along the line where rock met sand. There were the usual things—kelp draped like abandoned scarves, limpets clinging like tiny stubborn helmets, the occasional ruined crab exoskeleton (someone’s dinner, someone else’s warning). Tidepools caught the sky in their still surfaces, hosting small universes of weed and shell and darting, suspicious fish.

And there—just at the edge of where the beach tilted down into the more serious rocks—something glimmered.

Not the bright glare of sun on wet stone. This was a softer glow, pale and uncertain, the colour of old pearls or quiet moonlight.

Mara narrowed her eyes and stepped closer.

Shells.

They were arranged in a line along the sand, leading away across the uneven terrain like a string of beads dropped in a hurry. Each one was about the size of her thumb, delicately curved, their ridges smooth and intact. They were also, undeniably, faintly luminous.

Not enough to be dramatic. If she’d been out here at noon, she might not have seen it at all. But in the slant of late morning light, each shell seemed to hold a tiny smear of its own radiance, a ghost of glow that didn’t quite match the direction or colour of the sun.

The line of shells started a few feet from where she stood, as if whoever had laid them had begun well out of stomp-range. They wound between rocks, up over a small outcrop, and then disappeared from view behind a larger, weed-draped boulder.

She looked around, half expecting to see a child sprinting away giggling, or an artist lurking behind a dune waiting to see if anyone noticed their installation.

The beach was mostly empty. An old man with a metal detector was sweeping patient, hopeful arcs closer to the waterline. Two teenagers sat on a concrete breakwater, headphones in, mutually pretending the other didn’t exist. No one was paying her the slightest attention.

Mara’s scalp prickled.

She crouched to pick up the nearest shell.

It was cool, slick with a film of saltwater. The glow, up close, was more a suggestion than a fact—the faintest luminescence along the inner spiral, as if someone had breathed starlight into it and then mostly changed their mind. When she turned it over in her fingers, the sheen followed no logic of light source she understood.

“It’s bioluminescence,” she told herself. “Probably. Some tiny organisms. Or a trick of the angle. Or—”

The shell vibrated, very slightly, in her hand. Just a shiver, the way a cat’s skin twitches in a dream.

She nearly dropped it.

“Okay,” she said aloud, because sometimes sound steadied things. “New plan. We do not explain everything immediately, because that way lies headaches. We… observe.”

She set the shell back in its place in the line and straightened up.

The shells continued onward, their ghostly glow just enough to mark a path. It was such a deliberate arrangement. Too tidy for nature’s usual chaos, too random for the sort of pattern human children made when they were bored.

“Captain Thimble,” she muttered. “If this is one of your ‘interactive exhibits,’ I’m going to… continue to follow it, actually, because who am I kidding.”

She adjusted her backpack strap and stepped after the shells.

The rocks here were irregular, jutting and pocked. The sea’s retreat had left them slick in places, dry and rough in others. Mara picked her way carefully, feeling for stable footholds, testing each step. The line of shells twined ahead of her, occasionally disappearing behind a stone and then reappearing on the other side like a thought she kept almost losing.

As she moved farther from the main stretch of sand, the sound of the waves shifted in her ears, becoming more muffled, bounced and chopped by the surrounding rocks. Other noises rose in its place: the gurgle of trapped water, the clack of shells knocking together, the tiny fizz of dissolving foam.

And underneath all of that, something else.

A whispering, again—but closer now, clearer. Not in words she recognised, but with an intonation that felt like question, like invitation, like Come see.

She paused near a low ridge of stone. The shells led up and over it, their glow brightening just a fraction as the rock’s shadow fell across them. On the other side, she caught a glimmer of open water.

The ridge itself rose to around her waist. Seaweed had draped itself along its crest, drying to a dark, crackling fringe. Small barnacles clustered on its seaward face, the barnacle equivalent of a metropolis.

Mara put one hand on the rough rock for balance and scrambled clumsily up.

On the far side, the ground fell away more steeply than she’d expected, turning into a tangle of boulders worn into curves and channels by centuries of water. At the bottom of this tumble lay an isolated hollow, a basin scooped out of the stone.

A tidepool.

Plenty of tidepools dotted the lower beach, but this one was oddly set apart, as if the rest of the ocean had politely looked the other way when it reached this spot. The walls around it rose higher, making a little amphitheatre of stone. The pool itself was roughly oval and surprisingly still; even from here, Mari could see it wasn’t agitated by the usual patterns of surface ripples. It was a mirror punched into the world.

The line of shells descended the slope toward it, then circled its edge almost protectively, forming a loose, glowing halo.

Mara slid down the inner side of the ridge, catching herself with a hand against the rock. Her shoes skidded on patches of algae; tiny drops of cold water splashed her ankles. When she reached more level ground, she stood for a moment, just looking.

The pool’s water was clear, the colour of glass just before it takes on a tint. Bits of seaweed clung to the sides, swaying gently in water that did not appear to be moving. Sunlight slanted in, cutting clean lines on the stone.

She stepped closer until her toes edged the wet ring of stone at the pool’s lip. Instinctively, she expected to see the usual: sand and pebbles at the bottom, maybe a crab or two, perhaps a small fish darting about with paranoid efficiency.

Instead, the water held—her.

Her reflection stared back: damp curls escaping her ponytail, jacket hood shoved back, yesterday’s salt-scrubbed trainers, the faint smudge of pencil on her right hand that never quite went away. Above her, the same clear sky; around her, the same stones.

For a second, it was entirely ordinary.

Then the world shifted, very slightly. Like a picture behind glass catching a different angle of light.

Her reflection wavered, as if someone had breathed on the inside of the water.

The Mara in the pool blurred at the edges, colours smudging. The stones around her melted into softer, darker shapes. The sky behind her dimmed and—not blackened, exactly, but deepened, like the blue of the sea when you look straight down from a boat and remember there’s more below you than you like to think about.

Her reflected face peeled away, drifting, the features separating into ripples. For a breathless instant, there was nothing there but the suggestion of movement, the hint of depth.

Then something else resolved, where her reflection should have been.

It was not a simple picture. It had perspective, distance. It had light.

An archway hung there, made entirely of glow.

Not built of stone or metal, but of brightness itself: pale, wavering lines that traced the outline of a door with no apparent support. It was as if someone had drawn an opening in the air using phosphorescent ink.

The archway hovered a short distance “behind” the surface—behind, under, below; the directions were suddenly suspect—and beyond it, instead of more rock or sand, was darkness laced with faint threads of greenish light, moving slowly like currents or hanging weeds.

Every time the tidepool’s surface shivered, the arch’s outline flickered. It wasn’t a reflection of something above her; it was a window to something else.

Mara’s scalp prickled. Her breath had gone shallow without her noticing. She blinked twice, hard, then leaned closer, elbows against her knees, until her face was only a handspan above the water.

The arch remained.

She could make out the rough idea of a space beyond it—a sense of “downward,” of more water, of distance. Like looking down a well or a long, deep shaft.

The whispering at the edges of her hearing grew clearer. It felt like a sound you couldn’t hear with your ears, only your bones: the soft shiver of water over stone, the far-off thrum she’d felt in the museum wall, the echo of whale song thinned out for shore.

“Some doors stay open even when we pretend they’re shut,” Captain Thimble Kelp had said.

Her knees went weak.

“Door,” she whispered. The word tasted strange in this place. “You’re a door.”

The tidepool, being a tidepool, said nothing. But the arch seemed to brighten, just by a shade, as if someone had adjusted the contrast.

Mara’s mind did an unhelpful thing then: it offered her a series of perfectly sensible options.

You could go back, it suggested. You could go back up the rocks and onto the sand and pretend you never saw this. You could wait until the museum opens and tell Captain Thimble and let him deal with it. You could take a photo and think about it later. You could—

Her curiosity, which had never listened particularly well to reason and was currently very large and humming like a tensioned wire, overruled the list.

“Don’t you dare climb into that helmet,” Thimble had warned. He hadn’t said anything about tidepools.

“Mum is going to say I went looking for trouble,” she murmured. “It’s not my fault. Trouble left me a glowing trail of shells.”

Her hand hovered over the water.

It looked like water. It shone like water. A faint breeze from the sea ruffled its surface, distorting the view of the archway beneath. She could see, if she looked carefully, tiny bits of debris suspended in it: sand, the ghost-lace threads of plankton, a stray bubble.

“Just touch it,” she told herself. “That’s all. Touch the weird impossible thing and then… reassess.”

Captain Thimble’s voice echoed in the back of her mind: Some doors aren’t meant for children who ought to be home by supper.

Mara thought of the kitchen table. Of soggy cereal and the neighbour’s shed. Of homework waiting on her desk like a pile of folded, uninteresting storms. Then she thought of the slow, unfathomable pulse under her palm in the museum, the way her skin had prickled with something that was not fear exactly, but its cousin.

She dipped her fingers into the pool.

There was no splash. No chill shock. No resistance.

Her hand went through the surface as if through the thinnest layer of cool mist. For an instant, she felt only temperature—the same neutral cool of seawater.

Then something took hold of her.

Not in the sense of fingers or teeth. It was more like the sensation of a current catching your legs when you step into the shallows: that sly, sideways tug. A spiral of motion wound around her wrist, pressing inward and around, like a long, clear ribbon of stream pulling her in the direction it wanted to go.

She gasped.

The pull was not violent. It did not yank. It invited with steady, insistently growing pressure.

She tried to pull her hand back.

Her arm moved—slowly. There was a stretch, like trying to drag your hand through very thick syrup, but colder and impossibly smooth. The more she thought about resisting, the more the spiral seemed to tighten, coaxing rather than grabbing.

“Okay,” she said aloud, voice a little higher than she liked. “Okay, this is… different. This is new.”

The archway in the pool flared brighter, its edges sharpening. The darkness beyond it deepened to a richer blue-green, as if someone had turned the saturation up on an image of the sea at midnight.

Her balance shifted.

She realised, with a weirdly detached fascination, that the tug was no longer just around her wrist. It had crept up along her forearm, around her elbow, threading through her shoulder. It was rearranging which way was “down” for her, adjusting the labels on gravity.

The rocks under her feet felt less solid. The world tilted, not like slipping on ice, but like the moment in a dream when you step onto a stair that isn’t there and your stomach swoops.

Mara grabbed for the lip of the pool with her free hand. Her fingers found stone, rough and wet, barnacles biting shallow marks into her skin. She clung, breathing hard.

The water around her submerged arm felt… vast. That was the only word she had. Vast, as if what she’d plunged into was no longer a shallow bowl of trapped seawater but something that went on and on.

“Stop,” she whispered, though she wasn’t sure if she meant the current, or herself.

The sea hissed softly against the more distant shore. A gull called overhead, a harsh, mundane cry that sounded abruptly far away, as if shouted down a long tunnel.

Beneath her, through the strange bending of perspective in the pool, she saw it.

Below the glowing arch, below the plane of the water that wasn’t just water, something shimmered in the depths.

Lights.

Tiny ones, dozens, hundreds of them, scattered in three dimensions like fireflies that had learned to swim. They pulsed and drifted, some bright, some dim, arranged along what her mind insisted were structures: lines, gentle curves, vertical drops. A city seen from above at night, but instead of streets, the lights traced out something more organic, like veins or branches.

The sight twisted something in her chest. Awe, mostly. And a small, sharp spike of fear that pointed in entirely the wrong direction.

Down, it said. Down there.

“Of course,” Mara said hoarsely. “Of course there’s a glowing… whatever that is… under the weird door pool. Why not. Why be normal.”

Her hold on the stone lip slipped half an inch. Her submerged arm tugged harder, like a dog that has finally realised you really are going for a walk.

Captain Thimble’s voice came back, clear and exasperated in her memory: You’ve got that look. The one that asks ‘What if?’ louder than it asks ‘Should I?’.

“What if,” she said, “I don’t?”

The spiral of cool around her limbs pulsed, once. The lights below flared, then settled, patiently gleaming.

She pictured herself letting go. The current would pull. She would go through the arch. She might drown. She might not. She might fall forever. She might land.

She pictured turning back. Climbing up over the rocks, trying to explain to Captain Thimble later that she’d found a door and she’d… left it. The idea tasted like regret already, like a missed flavour she would never quite be able to describe.

“I am supposed to text if I change locations,” she muttered. “Does ‘somewhere under the ocean in a possibly imaginary glowing city’ count as a location?”

The universe, characteristically, did not provide clarification.

Her fingers hurt where they gripped the stone. The barnacles were not kind. The practical part of her brain, the one that liked safety and rules and oxygen, screamed something about foolishness and risk and lung capacity.

Her curiosity opened its mouth and swallowed that part whole.

“Fine,” she told the pool. “Fine. But if this goes badly, I’m haunting that museum.”

She took a careful breath, filling her chest, counting to three the way she did before jumping off the high diving board in the community pool that one summer when she’d decided she would not be the sort of person who always watched from the side.

One.

She loosened her grip on the stone.

Two.

She let her knuckles unclench, feeling the roughness slide past her fingertips.

Three.

She let go.

The world responded, not with a dizzy plummet, but with a decisive, impossible rearrangement.

Down was no longer the ground beneath her feet. Down was where her arm disappeared through the shining mirror of the tidepool. Gravity rotated ninety degrees like a dial being turned. Her stomach swept sideways. The edge of the pool, the rocks rimming it, the sky—they all tilted, sliding out of place.

Her feet left the rock with a sensation like stepping off a stair she hadn’t known was the last one. For a jagged instant she hung in a directionless space where up and down argued.

Then the water took her.

It was like being folded neatly but very quickly.

One moment, she was kneeling on the familiar, wet stone, half her arm submerged, sun on the back of her neck. The next, the world pivoted; her legs lifted; her body followed the line of her arm.

The pool’s surface wasn’t something she passed through so much as something that rotated around her. It slid from horizontal to vertical, a cold, slick curtain that wrapped and unwound.

There was no shock of plunging. No involuntary gasp, no rush of freezing. The temperature simply matched itself to her skin and went past it. Her hair lifted around her head, moving like seaweed. The little sounds of the beach—the gulls, the shush of waves on sand—stretched, thinned, and then snapped off, replaced by a deeper, more muffled hush.

Mara flailed for a second, instinctively reaching for something to hold. Her fingers closed on water and light.

She was not falling. Not exactly. She was… moving.

Down.

Above her—behind her?—the circular frame of the tidepool shrank, a bright, wavery disc. Through it, she saw, impossibly, the inverted image of the shore: rocks and sand and a wedge of sky, all turned wrong-side-up and far away, as if she were looking up from the bottom of a very deep well.

She kicked experimentally. Her legs met a medium that clung but did not drag, like swimming through silk. There was resistance, but it was gentle, more like someone’s hand on her back guiding her than water trying to hold her.

Her lungs, she realised with a jolt of alarm, were still full of air. She had taken that breath before letting go and… kept it. No water had rushed in. Her nose and mouth felt damp, but not flooded. The panic that had been coiling, waiting for the sudden need to breathe, faltered, confused.

Carefully, cautiously, she exhaled a little.

Tiny silver bubbles slipped from her lips, spinning lazily away. The sight was so strange and beautiful that she almost laughed, which would have been an excellent way to waste the rest of her air.

The tunnel around her glowed with its own, soft colours. The walls weren’t solid; they were water, all of it water, but denser, somehow, where the light thickened. Shafts of blue and green stretched downward, striped with paler bands like bars of sunlight reaching through clouds. Here and there, darker shapes swam—no, drifted—in the periphery of her vision: curtains of weed, perhaps, or shoals of fish that chose not to come too close.

The spiraling sensation hadn’t been an illusion. The current that held her turned, ever so slightly, around an axis she couldn’t see. She felt it in the way her orientation kept shifting: the distant circle of the surface moving from above her head to just behind her shoulder, then off to one side. She was descending and slowly corkscrewing, like a leaf in a gentle vortex.

Her backpack tugged at her shoulders, suddenly heavy, then oddly weightless. Its straps pressed reassuringly into her collarbones.

“Okay,” she thought, because talking out loud felt dangerous, as if words might be fragile in this place. “You’re going down through a tidepool. You are currently in a vertical tunnel made of water. This is either the best hallucination or the worst safety lecture.”

Below her, the lights she’d glimpsed from above grew bigger, brighter. They weren’t just dots anymore; they were clusters, lines, points of radiance strung along forms that suggested architecture, or at least organisation. Some glowed steady and soft; others blinked in slow pulses, like signals passed along a chain.

Her fear, which had been doing its level best to keep up with events, found itself outpaced.

In its place came a wonder so fierce it hurt.

Images tumbled through her thoughts: the dripping helmet in the museum, the maps with their shifting coastlines, the professor-sounding machines that had hummed with unspent questions, the pulse in the wall that had matched itself to her hand. The glowing door under the tide. The shells.

This is connected, she thought. This is all the same story.

The tunnel widened.

She felt it more than saw it—the subtle change in pressure, the way the current’s embrace loosened around her arms and legs. The blue-green light opened up ahead, growing less tubular and more… spacious.

Mara tilted her head back to look one last time.

Far, far above—or behind, or what used to be above—the circle of the tidepool was now no bigger than a coin held at arm’s length. Through it, she saw a smudge of sky and the faint suggestion of a human world, smaller and less certain than it had ever appeared from the beach.

Her chest ached, but not yet with need for air. With something else.

“Don’t close,” she thought at the shrinking circle of light. “Not yet. Please don’t close.”

It flickered, once, as if in acknowledgement. Or it might have been her own vision blurring.

Then the tunnel opened under her like a mouth.

The water around her thinned in that particular way that means space, the way the air in a high-ceilinged room feels different from that in a closet. The gradient of light shifted; blue gave way to softer teals, streaked with muted gold and the faint, translucent pink of phosphorescent creatures shifting in clusters.

The city lay below her.

There was no other word for it, not now that she could see it properly. It sprawled across the seafloor in a pattern that obeyed rules entirely its own.

Structures rose from the depths, some tall and narrow like chimneys, others broad and low like sleeping beasts. None of them were quite straight. They curved and flowed, built from what looked like a blend of coral and glass and something else altogether, something that carried its own light. Bridges arched between them in delicate, filigreed spans, studded with lanterns that dripped pale fire.

The lights she’d seen before were windows, doors, tiny bulbs on the tips of swaying fronds. They traced out streets that weren’t streets exactly, more like paths in three dimensions: up, across, through.

And all of it moved.

Not crumbling, not unstable, but… breathing. The buildings flexed in the almost-imperceptible way of living coral, opening and closing minuscule pores, shedding and renew­ing. Curtains of algae and strange, translucent banners fluttered in currents that she was only now beginning to feel teasing at her fingers.

Shapes darted between the glowing pillars—fish, yes, in flashes of silver and black and ridged blue, but also other forms: silhouettes that suggested arms as well as fins, tails that ended in fans instead of points, hair that drifted like seaweed.

Something huge and slow passed far below, its side dotted with soft lanterns, like a fragment of night sky deciding to go for a swim.

Mara hung there for a heartbeat in the gentle hold of the vertical current, suspended between the world she’d known and the one below. The circle of the shore above had grown smaller, paler, less persuasive by the second.

Her lungs gave a more assertive twinge. The thought rose again: breathe.

She looked down at the city of lights, heart pounding, and understood in some quiet, startled part of herself that whatever happened next, whoever she was when she returned—if she returned at all—would not be exactly the same girl who’d peered into a tidepool an hour ago, careful not to get her shoes too wet.

“Hello,” she thought at the luminous sprawl, at the impossible architecture and the drifting shapes within it. The word formed in her mind like a bubble.

Then, with the shore a shrinking coin above and the unknown blazing beneath, Mara loosened the last of her grip on the idea that doors should only open sideways, surrendered to the spiraling pull, and let herself be carried feet-first into the glowing ocean city waiting in the depths below.
