# Pelagoria, City of Glass and Currents

Where Mara arrives in the hidden city beneath the ocean and is both welcomed and arrested by its wary inhabitants.

Mara hit the air like a dropped feather.

One second, she was sliding down the glowing throat of the ocean, her hair floating around her in lazy, dark clouds and the weight of all that water holding her in a cool, constant embrace. The next, something invisible and elastic snapped around her, and she found herself tumbling—gently, but with conviction—through a space that was not water and was not exactly land.

Her stomach did a small, dignified somersault. Her shoes, which had been feeling very smug about being above all this wet nonsense, suddenly remembered gravity. She flailed, windmilling, as the tunnel of water above her sealed like an eye closing.

She fell perhaps three metres.

It was enough to make her yelp and, at the very last moment, to jam her eyes shut and think of every terrible thing that might be waiting for her at the bottom: sharp rocks, abandoned anchors, the historically inevitable skeleton.

Instead, she landed on something that felt like glass pretending to be stone.

Her knees buckled. She skidded, hands slapping down with a flat, ringing sound. Pain flared in her palms, sharp and clean, and then she was still, panting, her backpack thumping down beside her with a sullen flap.

For a moment, she stayed exactly where she was, folded over her elbows, breathing hard, trying to work out if she was alive or several interesting new pieces.

The surface under her palms was cool and faintly textured, like frosted glass. When she finally cracked one eye open, she saw—through the film of adrenaline and confusion—that it was pale as bleached bone, shot through with faint, glimmering lines where individual grains of sand had been melted and fused together. The pattern reminded her of waves frozen mid-crest.

She lifted her head.

The walkway she’d landed on arched gently away in both directions, a pale ribbon suspended in a space that should not exist.

Above her, there was no ceiling—not in any traditional, health-and-safety sense. Instead, the air ended abruptly in a curved, invisible surface that bulged outward in all directions, like the inside of a glass sphere. Beyond that thin, perfect boundary, the ocean pressed close.

Mara could see it as clearly as if she were standing inside a colossal, exquisitely clean fishbowl: endless blue-green, streaked with dim shafts of light from some faraway surface; shoals of tiny silver fish that fluttered past like handfuls of thrown confetti; larger, slower shapes gliding at the edges of visibility, careful and watchful. Dark fronds of kelp rose and swayed, their tips brushing the outside of the bubble as if testing it.

The bubble itself—this bubble of air she was breathing—hung in the water like a pearl. It didn’t wobble. It didn’t leak. It simply… was.

Mara’s mouth went dry. Which was impressive, given where she was.

The walkway beneath her continued, one end vanishing into a luminous haze, the other curving toward something that filled her field of vision when she dared to look.

The city.

Pelagoria.

It rose from the seafloor in a sweep of geometry that ought to have been impossible and, she felt instinctively, refused to care.

She could see domes—dozens of them, hundreds, some small as houses, some vast as stadiums—clustered together like bubbles of glass, each one holding its own pocket of stolen sky. Their surfaces caught and refracted the underwater light, turning it into shifting rainbows that slid across their curves. Between them, bridges arched and coiled: some as solid and pale as the walkway beneath her, others more translucent, rippling and flexing with the lazy grace of water tamed into shapes.

Spirals of coral rose among the domes: towers that curled like nautilus shells, walls riddled with windows that shone from within. Some grew straight from the rock; others appeared to be anchored to nothing at all, held in place by braids of living seaweed and beams of pale light.

Long, trailing banners of some thin, shimmering fabric fluttered from the tallest structures, stirring in invisible currents. They were the colours of deep ocean things: midnight blues, soft greens, the flushed pink of certain shells. Symbols were stitched along their lengths in thread that caught the light like fish scales.

It was not a neat city. It hadn’t been laid out with straight lines and obedient grids. It swooped and dipped, squabbled with itself, piled buildings up in haphazard layers that still, somehow, made sense. It looked like it had grown, like coral or a particularly ambitious story, adding bits over centuries wherever there was the slightest space.

Tiny lights pricked its depths: lanterns hung in windows, glowing nodes along the bridges, clusters of luminescence in garden-like pockets where tall, translucent plants waved slowly. Schools of fish threaded themselves through its alleys, flashing silver and blue. Far beneath, a shadow the size of a cathedral coasted past, lanterns along its flank winking solemnly as it moved.

Mara’s brain threw words at the sight like pebbles into deep water: incredible, unbelievable, impossible. None of them made a dent.

She realised dimly that she was still on her hands and knees on the walkway and that her mouth was, in fact, hanging open.

She shut it, because some inner voice reminded her that one ought to appear at least slightly composed when arriving in a hidden undersea city in a bubble.

Her lungs were starting to protest the breath she’d taken before leaving the tunnel. She gingerly tried inhaling.

Air filled her chest. Cool, crisp, faintly tasting of salt and something metallic, like it had passed over a lot of rock and science on its way to her.

She coughed once, half in surprise.

“Right,” she said, out loud, because if you could talk, then things were probably not as terrible as they might otherwise be. Her voice rang oddly, bouncing back and forth in the bubble. “Okay. So. Not dead. That’s… that’s good.”

The walkway vibrated, a subtle tremor under her palms, as if something large had just stepped onto it some way off.

Mara froze.

The tremor became a rhythm: a series of approaching, uneven thuds and lighter, scraping sounds. Not the randomness of distant movement. Footsteps. Several sets of footsteps. Armoured ones.

She pushed herself unsteadily to her feet. Her knees wobbled. The bubble’s air felt suddenly thinner.

At first, all she could make out was motion at the cityward end of the walkway: a ripple of darker shapes against the brilliance beyond. Then, out of that haze, five figures resolved, striding toward her in a loose formation that gave the impression of people who had absolutely done this before and were not expecting to enjoy it.

They were… people. Mostly.

They walked upright, on two legs, with the broad-shouldered, purposeful gait of guards everywhere. They wore armour that caught the light in sudden, startling flashes: plates and scaled pieces that curved over torsos, shoulders, and thighs, made of something that looked like mother-of-pearl forged into discipline. It was pale in some places, darker in others, patterned with faint ripples that moved when they moved.

Where their skin showed at elbows, throats, and cheeks, it gleamed faintly. Scales, Mara realised. Tiny, overlapped like the finest chain mail, in colours that shifted between green and blue and the soft, bruised purple of storm clouds over water.

Their hair flowed behind them like streamers, unfurling in the slight currents of air inside the bubble: some dark as Mara’s, some pale as sand, some a startling, nacreous white. Here and there, thin fins protruded delicately from forearms or along jawlines, twitching as if tasting the air.

In their hands, they carried tridents.

Mara had seen tridents in pictures: the sort of thing drawn in the hands of gods who were fond of storms and boundary issues. Those had looked theatrical. These did not.

These were long and slender, made of some pale, gleaming metal that was not quite silver, their three tines tapering to points that looked perfectly capable of conducting a strong moral argument directly to someone’s spleen. Veins of faint, bluish light ran along the shafts, pulsing gently, in time with nothing Mara could identify.

As they came closer, their faces resolved: high cheekbones, wide eyes set a little farther apart than humans’, irises in improbable colours—sea-glass green, sunburst gold, stormy grey. Their expressions were a fascinating mixture of alarm, wariness, and the particular sort of excitement that came from a day suddenly deciding not to be boring after all.

The one in front raised a hand. The others fanned out, forming a half-circle around Mara with smooth, practised efficiency.

Mara’s first thought, which arrived with the shrill clarity of panic, was that she had been here all of thirty seconds and she was already surrounded by heavily armed fish-people.

Her second thought was that they were not, in fact, fish-people. They were something else. Something in-between.

Her third thought was, unhelpfully: They’re very shiny.

The lead guard—taller than the others, with a crest of short, silvery fin along one temple and a long scar trailing from the corner of their mouth down under their jaw—levelled their trident. Not quite at her, but close enough that Mara became acutely aware of how soft and entirely un-scaled her own throat was.

They spoke.

The words came out in a rush of sound that was both familiar and utterly alien. There was a cadence to it, a rolling rhythm that made her think of waves breaking on different kinds of shore: some syllables short and bright, like pebbles; others long and burbling, full of swallowed consonants and round vowels that seemed to curl back on themselves.

Mara’s ears heard: “Kesh thalan varu’ith? Sel a’se, sel a’se!”

Her brain heard: seabirds and bubble streams, someone humming through a snorkel.

She blinked. “I’m sorry?”

The guard frowned. The fin along their jaw twitched. They tried again, more slowly, stabbing the air with one word in particular and punctuating it with a jab of the trident in her direction.

“Sur-fa-ce.”

That, at least, made it through the translation barrier. The way they said it made it sound like a diagnosis.

“Yes,” Mara said cautiously. “Surface. Me. Hi.”

She lifted one hand in what she hoped was a non-threatening, universal gesture of greeting and not, in this place, some horrible insult involving sea cucumbers.

The guard’s eyes flicked to her fingers, then back up. Their nostrils flared, their pupils tightening for a fraction of a second. They barked a word over their shoulder. Two of the other guards shifted their grip on their tridents, sliding half a step closer, as if she’d just brandished a small nuclear device instead of five calloused fingers and bitten-down nails.

One of them muttered something that was probably not “Oh good, a child, what a relief.”

Mara swallowed.

“Right,” she said, words coming out faster, higher. Nervous babbling, her old friend. “So. You don’t know what I’m saying, I don’t know what you’re saying, and if this is anything like human security, that means eventually someone is going to start shouting, and I don’t—I really don’t think we need to shout. I didn’t mean to—”

She cut herself off as another voice cut through the bubble.

This one was clearer, firmer, threaded with the sort of authority that made people straighten their backs without consulting their opinions.

“Stand down. She’s not armed.”

The guards shifted as one, tridents lowering just a fraction, not enough to stop being a threat, but enough to admit that perhaps stabbing the small, dripping stranger might not be the immediate first step.

A new figure approached along the walkway from the city’s direction.

Even among people with opalescent skin and hair that behaved like the dream of shampoo advertisements, she stood out.

She moved with a slow, assured grace, as if every step was a decision worth making properly. Her robe—if robe was the right word for the garment that draped from her shoulders in ripples of colour—was made of something that looked like liquid light woven into cloth. It clung and flowed and never seemed quite to agree on what hue it wanted to be. At one angle it was deep-sea green; at another, the pale blue of shallow water; in the corner of Mara’s eye, she could have sworn it flashed with the fiercer blue-white of breaking waves.

Around her shoulders, strands of living coral coiled in delicate loops, forming a collar and then flowing upward into a crown. Tiny polyps along their lengths opened and closed rhythmically, their minuscule tentacles tasting the air. Here and there, small, bioluminescent creatures—perhaps symbiotic, perhaps adornment, perhaps both—glowed softly, studded in the coral like jewels.

Her skin was a deep, resonant brown under the sheen of fine, almost invisible scales. Her hair floated in a steady halo around her head, dark and thick, threaded with the occasional glint of shell or tiny silver fish that had decided it was the safest place in the ocean.

Her eyes, when they fixed on Mara, were the dark, complex colour of tide pools at dusk: layers of greens and blues and flecks of gold shifting behind a cool surface.

Mara did not need an introduction to know who she was.

Some people wore authority like a badge or a uniform. This woman wore it the way the sea wore waves: as a default condition.

The lead guard straightened, thumping the butt of their trident lightly against the walkway. The sound rang out, a clean, bell-like note.

“Your Majesty,” they said, in their rolling, burbling tongue. Even without translation, the title was clear.

Queen Nerissa of Pelagoria regarded the child who had fallen into her city as if she were a particularly complicated tide chart.

Up close, Mara became acutely aware of how small and scruffy she must look. Her jacket was damp and patched at one elbow. Her jeans were scuffed and bore the faint ghosts of old grass stains. Her shoes were, as ever, betraying their cheapness around the seams. Salt had dried in pale streaks on her cheeks. Her hair, which she had optimistically tied back that morning, had rebelled and formed a small, drifting cloud around her head.

She pulled herself up as straight as she could, for no other reason than that slouching felt like losing.

Queen Nerissa stepped to the front of the half-circle of guards. She did not reach for a weapon. She did not snap commands. She simply stood and looked.

Mara, who knew full well what it was to feel like an odd piece in a puzzle no one else could see, held that gaze.

For a moment that stretched long and taut, nothing moved. Even the ever-present fish beyond the bubble seemed to have paused, hanging in the water like commas.

Then Nerissa spoke.

Her voice was lower than Mara had expected, softer, like the sound of distant surf heard from inside a cave. The language was the same as the guards’, full of burred consonants and liquid vowels, but there was an oldness in it, a gravity.

She said several words. Mara caught, unmistakably, “surface” and something that was probably “child.”

The lead guard answered, adding a series of phrases punctuated with expressive hand gestures: a fall, a door, a tunnel, a this-just-came-out-of-the-sky-and-we-are-not-thrilled.

Queen Nerissa’s eyes never left Mara’s face. She tilted her head slightly, considering. Then, with a small, abrupt gesture, she flicked her fingers in front of her throat, as if snapping an invisible thread.

Something in the air hummed.

It was faint, like the crackle before lightning, or the sound old televisions used to make when they were on in the next room. A prickle ran along Mara’s arms, raising goosebumps. She became acutely aware of her tongue, her teeth, the shape of her own mouth.

Nerissa spoke again, this time slowly, each syllable deliberate.

“Can you understand me now, child of the dry lands?”

The words were in clear, precise English. They sat oddly in the Queen’s mouth—not clumsy, but as if each had been picked up, turned over, and chosen for weight as well as sound.

Mara’s jaw dropped a little. She shut it again.

“Yes,” she said. Her own voice sounded thin compared to Nerissa’s. “I—I can. What was—how—?”

Nerissa made a small, graceful gesture with one hand, as though batting the question aside like a drifting piece of seaweed.

“Translation shell,” she said. “A net for meaning. It sits in the air between your words and ours, if I will it. There are… old agreements.” A faint, wry twist touched the corner of her mouth. “They assumed more polite visits than this.”

She took a step closer, the coral of her crown glinting.

“You fell through one of our doors,” she said. It wasn’t quite a question.

Mara wet her lips, suddenly very aware that every movement she made was being watched by five armed guards and a ruling monarch.

“Yes,” she said. “I mean—I didn’t mean to. I was on the beach. There was a tidepool. With a… sort of glowing arch thing? And shells, that were kind of… luminous? And I touched it, and then it—well, it decided I was coming, I think, because I tried not to, but currents are very insistent and—”

She heard herself and clamped down hard before the babbling reached high tide.

“I found a door,” she said, forcing the words to line up neatly. “And I fell through it. I didn’t know it led here. I didn’t even know ‘here’ was… was real.”

Nerissa’s eyes narrowed the smallest fraction. “And yet,” she said, “you walked into it.”

Mara opened her mouth. Closed it again. Honesty, she suspected, would not make her look better. Nevertheless, it came out.

“Yes,” she admitted. “I… I was curious.”

One of the guards made a small, incredulous noise. Another said, in their own language, something that the translation net rendered in Mara’s head as, “Of course she was.”

“Curious,” Nerissa repeated, tasting the word. “Curious. That is a dangerous current.”

Mara bristled, just a little. “It’s not a bad thing,” she said. “Not by itself.”

Nerissa tilted her head, and for a heartbeat, something almost like humour flickered behind her eyes. “No,” she said quietly. “By itself, it is only movement. But currents reshape coasts.”

Her gaze travelled over Mara from head to toe, noting details: the cheap, worn clothes, the ink smudges on her fingers, the stubborn set of her jaw. Her eyes lingered, briefly, on the faint white salt-crystal still caught at the edge of Mara’s thumbnail, a relic from the museum floor that had survived storms, tunnels, and transitions.

“You came from the surface without a suit,” Nerissa said. “Without a guide. Without so much as a breath-stone.” She shook her head slowly, a small ripple passing through her hair. “You should not be here. And yet… here you are. Alive.”

“That wasn’t entirely my idea,” Mara muttered. “The door was very… persuasive.”

Nerissa’s mouth tightened. “The door should not have opened at all,” she said. “Not to one of you.”

She turned slightly, addressing the lead guard without taking her attention off Mara for long.

“Captain Tideglass. How long from the first alarm-shell until her arrival?”

The guard—Tideglass—shifted, trident held against their shoulder with practised ease.

“Three hundred heartbeats, Majesty,” they replied. “Give or take the panic.”

“Long enough for her to drown or be crushed,” Nerissa said. “Yet she is neither. Long enough for the Tunnel to close.” She flicked a glance upwards, to where the vertical gouge of water that had delivered Mara was now a faint shimmer in the ceiling of the bubble, already knitting itself closed like a wound. “And yet it did not. It held for her.”

The way she said “her” made Mara feel simultaneously very important and very much like a problem.

“I didn’t mean to trespass,” Mara said, because the word surfacer had come wrapped in an accusation. “I didn’t know. I was just—on the beach. I saw the shells, and the light, and I followed, and—”

“And the sea opened her hand to you,” Nerissa finished softly, eyes darkening. “When she has been keeping her fists tight for years.”

She was silent for a moment, weighing something invisible. The guards shifted slightly, the faint chime of their armour a delicate percussion.

“This city,” Nerissa said, her voice carrying more than just sound now, “is hidden for a reason, child from above. We live in the cracks between your world and the deep. We walk in air and swim in water. We exist because we are forgotten. That forgetting is our safety. The last time a door like that yawned wide, we nearly lost all of this.”

Her hand swept out, encompassing the domes, the spirals, the tiny, gleaming lives drifting through them.

Mara’s chest clenched. “Then why—”

“Why did it open for you?” Nerissa’s gaze speared her. “That is precisely what I intend to find out.”

There was a rustle among the guards. One of them, shorter than the rest, their scales edged with a faint rose-gold sheen, murmured, “Your Majesty, she is only a child.”

“Children drown as easily as their elders,” Nerissa said, not unkindly. “And innocent feet trample coral just the same as guilty ones.” She let out a breath, the sound of a tide retreating over pebbles.

“But,” she added, and there was a subtle shift in the air, a loosening of something, “children also see what the old have trained themselves to ignore.”

Her eyes returned to Mara’s face with a new, sharper intent.

“Tell me your name,” she said.

Mara had to stop herself from giving the name in full, with middle, as she would if a teacher or doctor asked. This was not a form. This was a queen.

“Mara Bright,” she said. Her voice was steadier now than she felt. “From… well, from up there.”

She jerked her chin toward the vague direction of the surface, or at least where she thought the surface ought to be. In Pelagoria, the concept of “up” had already started to feel negotiable.

Nerissa repeated the name quietly, as if hooking it into memory.

“Mara Bright from Above,” she said. “You stand in Pelagoria without leave or knowledge. You have crossed boundaries older and deeper than you can yet understand. By our laws, that makes you a trespasser.”

The word hung in the air, heavy as an anchor.

Mara swallowed. “I—”

“However,” Nerissa went on, overriding whatever Mara had been about to say with the effortless authority of someone who had never in her life had to shout to be heard, “by those same laws, a trespasser who arrives by way of the Queen’s Door”—she nodded upward, toward the now-faded scar in the bubble—“is under my protection until I know what to do with them.”

That was… better? Possibly? Mara’s experience with legal phrasing was limited to terms and conditions she did not read before clicking, but “protection” seemed preferable to “immediate imprisonment” or “feeding to enormous symbolic fish.”

Nerissa turned to Captain Tideglass.

“Captain, you and your squad will escort this surface-child to the palace,” she said. “Gently. No points, no chains. She is not to be harmed unless she proves herself a danger.”

Tideglass inclined their head. The scar along their jaw pulled tight. “Majesty,” they said, the syllables crisp and reluctant.

“And send word to Professor Finn,” Nerissa added, almost as an afterthought, though the slight roll of her eyes suggested it was not as simple as that. “Tell him the sea has delivered him a… puzzle. He may wish to contain his excitement until after I’ve spoken with the girl. But he won’t.”

She looked back to Mara, and for the first time, something softened in her expression. It was not quite kindness. It was more like recognition.

“You will come with us,” she said. “You will answer our questions. You will not run, or shout, or touch anything that looks like it took more than ten years to grow.”

Mara had an unhelpful flash of herself tripping and taking out an entire cultural landmark. “I didn’t come here to hurt anything,” she said. “I just… wanted to see.”

“Yes,” Nerissa murmured. “That is what I am afraid of.”

She gave a small, almost imperceptible signal with two fingers. The guards shifted, their formation loosening just enough to fall into an escort rather than a threat.

Two fell in beside Mara, one on each side, their tridents now held upright, the faint glow along the shafts dimming as if in response to a change of mood. Tideglass took point; another guard brought up the rear, casting occasional, quick glances at Mara as if half expecting her to suddenly explode into teeth.

“Walk,” Tideglass said tersely.

Mara hesitated, casting one more look upward.

The tunnel she had come through was nearly gone, its once-bright shaft blending back into the blue ceiling of the bubble. Beyond that, through the curved transparency, the sea stretched away in all directions, vast and unreadable.

Somewhere very far above, there was a shore with a little town and a green, flaking door to a museum that shouldn’t exist. Somewhere even higher, there was sky.

She squared her shoulders, the way she did when stepping into classrooms that smelled of marker pens and other children’s noise.

“Okay,” she said quietly, more to herself than anyone else. “Palace. Questions. Not touching anything.”

She walked.

The fused-sand walkway had a faint give to it, like very dense rubber. It curved toward the city in a gentle, confident arc, the pale path leading her past wonders she had no vocabulary for yet.

As they advanced, the scale of Pelagoria shifted. What had been an impressionistic sweep of domes and towers resolved into detail.

She saw people moving within the nearer bubbles: children racing along narrow walkways, their bare, scale-sheened feet slapping rhythmically; an older figure perched on a low wall, mending a net that shivered and reformulated itself with each knot; someone hanging a string of glowing shells along the edge of a balcony, whistling a tune that made Mara’s skin pebble even through the translation net.

Bridges of water arched beneath and above her: shimmering bands that looked like streams plucked up and stitched between platforms. Within them, fish moved in both directions, some darting through the thin layer of liquid as if it were a highway, others pausing to hover and then shoot off at sudden angles. The bridges adjusted themselves minutely as currents changed, flexing but never breaking.

Closer to the domes, the walkway branched. One spur dipped downward toward a great, transparent arch embedded in the side of the largest bubble, through which Mara could see an enormous open space, full of movement and colour. Another spur curled up to a smaller, more ornate entrance, flanked by tall columns of living coral carved—or grown—together into spirals.

They took the upward path.

As she walked, Mara felt eyes on her.

People—Pelagorians? Pelagori?—had come to the edges of their bubbles and balconies, drawn by the sight of the guards and their unlikely prize. Some peered through the clear walls of the domes, their faces flattened slightly by the curvature, like fish in reverse. Others leaned against railings of braided shell and kelp, their expressions variously wary, fascinated, or unmistakably disapproving.

Mara caught snatches of their speech, the translation shell doing its best.

“…it’s true, a surface-child…”

“…after all these years…”

“…Queen’s Door, they say…”

“…should have been sealed…”

“…maybe the stories—”

A younger voice, bright with unfiltered excitement: “Do you think she’s got gills hiding under that cloth?”

“No, idiot, they can’t breathe properly down here. Look, her lips are going pale.”

Mara resisted the urge to clap a self-conscious hand over her mouth. She could feel the thinness of the air now—the way each breath filled her lungs, but not as easily as on land. There was something… thinner about it. Like air up a mountain, maybe, she thought, though she’d only ever seen mountains in books.

The escort turned onto the last curve of the walkway, leading toward the ornate entrance.

Above it, carved into the fused-sand arch in curling, flowing lines that almost looked alive, were words in that same flowing script she’d seen on the museum’s strange maps. The translation net murmured in her ear: PELAGORIA HIGH HALL. HOUSE OF THE TIDEBORN QUEEN.

Mara had just enough time to think that this was a lot to live up to before the world shifted perspective again.

High above the archway, half-hidden in curving shadows and the leaves of some enormous, fanlike plant, a small balcony jutted from the side of a coral tower.

On it, a girl pressed herself against the railing, fingers curled around the smooth, ridged surface. Her hair, dark and heavy, drifted around her head, refusing to decide which way was down. Faint iridescent scales glittered along her arms, where the light caught them under the thin weave of her tunic. Delicate fins fringed the outsides of her forearms, held tight against her skin for now; another thin crest ran from just behind one ear, disappearing into her hair.

Her eyes, though—her eyes were human-brown, deep and watchful.

She had been there for some time, pretending, to anyone who glanced up, to be admiring the banners or checking the condition of the higher coral. In reality, she had been listening.

The words carried differently up here, fuzzed and bent by the curve of the bubble, but the tone—especially Nerissa’s—came clear. The guards’ alarm. The word surfacer, hissed with old fear. Her mother’s measured anger.

The girl had heard the name too: Mara Bright. It did not sound like the names she knew. It sounded like someone trying to start a story mid-sentence.

She squinted down now at the little procession making its way across the final span of walkway. The child at its centre was smaller than she’d expected. All the stories made surfacers sound huge, loud, the sort of people you could hear coming three currents away. This one looked… ordinary. Off-balance. Her clothes hung wrong, made for air that pressed differently.

But there was something in the way she held her shoulders, as if every part of her wanted to flinch and refused to.

The girl’s fingers tightened on the balcony rail until the knuckles blanched faintly under the thin scale-sheen.

“So,” she whispered to herself, so softly even the nearest fish could not quite catch it. “You made it through a door that shouldn’t open.”

Her voice had the dry edge of someone used to not being believed and making peace with that through sarcasm.

“Finally,” Liora of the Glass Tides murmured, human-brown eyes very wide as she watched Mara vanish into the cool, gleaming shadow of the palace entrance. “Finally, one of the old stories decided to be true.”
