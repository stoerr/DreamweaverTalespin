# Weaving the New Shield

Where the entire city participates in a daring ritual to reshape the shield while Mara balances between two worlds.

Pelagoria had never been this quiet.

Even on the calmest days, the city carried noise the way waves carried light: the tick of workshops, the rush of water through channels, the rumble of distant pumps, the overlapping murmur of thousands of voices layered over the constant, low heartbeat of the sea.

Today, it felt as if the ocean had inhaled and forgotten how to exhale.

The great central plaza, usually a place of bright chaos—market stalls spilling shells and woven weed, children chasing flickerfish in the narrow water-lanes, elders arguing amiably over currents and recipes—had been cleared.

No stalls, no stray nets, no banners hawking smoked eel or glimmer-pearls. Just the smooth, pale floor of fused sand, swept so clean it looked almost new, and the people of Pelagoria standing around its edge in wide, careful rings.

They weren’t pressing forward, the way surface crowds did in Mara’s world when something big was about to happen. There was distance between them, small gaps like breaths left between words. But there were a lot of them. Their presence filled the plaza more than any stalls could have.

Mara stood beside Queen Nerissa on the lowest step of the plaza’s raised central platform, with Liora on Nerissa’s other side and Professor Finn a little behind, shifting from foot to foot in nervous, off-beat time.

From here, the circles of people looked like ripples.

Closest to the platform: guards in pale, scaled armour, Captain Tideglass at their head, tridents butt-down, faces set in the careful neutrality of people determined not to admit they were afraid. Behind them: technicians and engineers with tool-belts slung low, their fingers twitching, eyes fixed on the machine that towered behind Nerissa. Beyond that, in widening rings, the regular citizens—shopkeepers with calloused hands, builders with coral dust in their hair, fishers with gill-scars along their wrists, children pressed against parents’ sides.

Faces lifted toward the platform: wary, hopeful, resigned, skeptical. And here and there, little gleams of something sharper. Curiosity. Defiance. The same things that hummed under Mara’s own skin.

They were all looking at the same focal point.

The shield-engine.

Mara had seen it before in Finn’s cluttered workshop, squeezed against the back wall like some overgrown, sulking pet. Here, in the open air of the plaza, given room and a proper foundation, it was magnificent and terrifying in equal measure.

It spiraled up from a broad base of fused sand and coral, like a giant shell unfurling. Its main ribs were thick arcs of translucent material, curved around a central column of braided shell and metal. Between the ribs hung membranes of water held in impossible sheets and twisting tubes, shot through with soft, restless light. Gears turned slowly within those watery curtains—some as small as Mara’s thumb, others the size of cartwheels—shell teeth glinting, centres pulsing with captured glow.

Coral grew along its spine, not as decoration but as living conduit. Tiny polyps opened and closed in waves, tasting the air, feeding on light. Thin veins of metal, etched with runes that glowed faintly, wrapped around those coral outgrowths, binding the biological and the mechanical into a single, uneasy whole.

At the very top, a crown of delicate branches arched outward, each tipped with a cluster of listening shells—little spirals like cupped ears—angled toward the dome above, toward the unseen storm.

The whole thing thrummed.

Not yet the great, over-loud hum that had strained the Whale’s lanterns, but a low, unsettled vibration. Caged stormlight flickered along its membranes, flashes of too-bright white caught and softened as they ran through spirals and tubes.

Nerissa stepped forward. Her robes, layered in soft greens and sea-blues, flowed behind her like gentle backwash between rocks. The coral of her crown had been braided with strands of duller, matte twig-coral today—less ornamental, more grounding.

She laid one hand on the Engine’s lowest rib.

The flickering light steadied, just a fraction.

“All right,” Professor Finn breathed, barely audible over the soft, collective rustle of the crowd shifting. “She likes you. That’s… something.”

Mara’s fingers ached with the urge to draw—this whole scene, the Engine’s towering spiral, Nerissa’s small, steady figure before it, Liora’s tense shoulders, the wide rings of people—but there was nowhere to put pencil or paper. She curled her hands into fists at her sides instead, ink-stains and salt-crystals pressing into her palms.

Above them, beyond the clear curve of the dome, the sea moved.

They could see it through the shell overhead: a huge, uneven weight of darkened water, streaked with pale, agitated curls. Occasionally, the far-off flash of lightning turned the whole membrane briefly into a mirror, showing back the city’s own upturned faces.

Farther out, beyond even the dome’s reach, a faint, slow-moving constellation of lights drifted along the deep—the Lantern Whale, keeping station near the Veil’s outer edge. His lanterns were dim today, their glow banked to a solemn murmur.

He was watching.

Mara could feel it, not with eyes or ears but with that new, taut sense in her bones she was only just beginning to understand: the awareness of something vast, patient, and currently very intent, hanging out in the dark like a thought at the edge of words.

Queen Nerissa lifted her hand from the Engine and turned to face the crowd.

“People of Pelagoria,” she said.

She didn’t shout. She didn’t need to. The plaza was built to carry her voice—stone and coral and water cooperating to pass the sound along, softening it without ever letting it fade.

“You know why we are here,” she went on. “You have felt it. In your walls. In your bones. In the Whale’s song. The Veil that has hidden us for a generation is straining. The storms above grow sharper. The deep below grows… restless.”

A murmur, low and uneasy, rippled through the rings.

“We built this Engine,” Nerissa said, resting her hand once more on the spiral of shell and living coral, “to keep us safe. We asked Madam Ocean to let us hum along her skirts instead of being swept with every tide. We asked the Lantern Whale to turn his songs around us instead of over us. We wove our fear of nets and cages and poison into its workings. And it did what we asked.”

She paused. The words hung, dense as water.

“But fear does not know when to stop,” she said softly. “It sings the same note until it drowns all others. It has made the Veil too tight. It has hurt those we did not mean to hurt. It has begun to crack the very shell we live in.”

Her gaze moved across the crowd. Mara followed it, seeing the flickers of guilt, of grief, of stubborn denial.

“Today,” Nerissa said, “we will loosen it.”

That word—loosen—landed like a dropped weight. Shoulders tightened. Fins twitched. Someone at the edge of one ring hissed, “Foolish,” under their breath.

Nerissa heard it. She inclined her head slightly.

“Perhaps,” she said. “Perhaps this is foolish. But I have seen what our cautious wisdom has brought. I will take a foolish hope over a careful collapse.”

She stepped aside then, gesture subtle but unmistakable, making space at the front of the platform.

Liora swallowed, throat bobbing.

Mara felt, absurdly, like someone had just shoved her out onto a stage with no script. Her palms went damp.

Professor Finn cleared his throat and stepped forward just enough to be seen by the technicians clustered nearest the Engine.

“Right,” he said, voice pitching up awkwardly at the first syllable before he wrestled it back down. “You all know your stations. Outer valve circle—you’re on intake modulation. If you see pressure spikes, you bleed them sideways, not down. I don’t care what your uncle taught you; today we’re not treating problems like enemies to punch.”

A ripple of strained laughter. The tension in the nearest ring eased by a hair.

“Inner shell-tuners,” Finn went on, “you listen for Madam’s reply. If she shoves, you… say ‘thank you’ and move with it. Do not attempt to out-stubborn the sea. She has more practice.”

“Heartstone team,” he said, nodding toward a group of stolid-looking builders across from him, each with a long, thin metal rod strapped to their backs like a lance. “You know where you’re going. When I signal, you plant those rods on your assigned stones and hold them there. Doesn’t matter what it feels like. Doesn’t matter if your teeth rattle and your ears buzz. That’s the point.”

“What if we pass out?” someone called.

“Then you’ll be unconscious and I’ll be annoyed,” Finn said. “Try not to. Breath-stones are ready if anyone gets too… poetic.”

“Everyone else,” he said, sweeping an arm to take in the concentric rings, “you’re chorus. Think of this Engine as a very large, very temperamental instrument, and yourselves as its… tuning forks.”

A few children in the second ring looked instantly delighted by the idea of being trusted to vibrate on purpose.

“You all know songs,” Finn said. “Work chants. Lullabies. Tide-calls. We’re going to give the Engine some of those patterns, instead of just numbers and ‘NO.’ Don’t worry if you’re not ‘good singers.’ The sea does not care if you are on pitch. It cares if you are… earnest.”

He glanced sideways at Mara, at Liora.

“And we have… new patterns to add,” he said. “From above. And from between.”

Nerissa touched his arm lightly. “Begin,” she said.

He nodded once and stepped back, leaving the front of the platform fully to Liora and Mara.

Liora exhaled.

“I hate being watched,” she muttered out of the corner of her mouth.

“Same,” Mara said. “On land, I hide behind bookshelves.”

“We’ll have to build more bookshelves after this,” Liora said. “For hiding. And storing complaints.”

She stepped forward.

The plaza held its breath.

Liora drew in a long, steadying inhalation, filling lungs that had never known air alone until Mara arrived. Her fingers curled and flexed at her sides, as if unsure whether to reach for stone, for water, or for a hand.

Then she sang.

The first note fell into the silence like a drop into still water.

It was clear and low, not the high, bright tones Mara had heard in some Pelagorian songs. It sat in Liora’s chest and ribs, a sound that belonged as much to bone as to throat. It swelled, carrying easily across the plaza, up into the dome, down into the ground.

The melody that followed was old.

Mara could feel that, even without knowing the words. It moved in slow arcs, dipping and rising with the laziness of a noonday tide. There were strange intervals—notes that didn’t quite fit Mara’s ear, held a hair longer than she expected, slipping into others by narrow, sliding steps. But there was also a familiarity there, like the way waves always, eventually, returned to shore.

The words, translated by the net, brushed against Mara’s mind like seaweed in a gentle current:

Stone below me, salt around,  
Walls that grew from empty ground,  
Harbour hidden, harbour home,  
Where the lost and tired come.

Fins that folded, feet that slipped,  
Lanterns hung in currents’ grip,  
Here we anchor, here we hide,  
Here we breathe between the tides.

The Engine’s hum responded.

It didn’t change entirely; this was no instant transformation. But it… noticed. A faint overtone crept into the low thrum, a new note that lined itself up under Liora’s, trying to match and not quite knowing how.

The listening shells along the Engine’s crown tilted imperceptibly toward her.

Mara’s chest ached.

She thought of her own home: not a carved coral dome in an impossible bubble, but a semi-detached house with peeling paint and a temperamental boiler, three streets back from the sea. The smell of damp towels, toast, and recycled air. The little square of garden where nothing quite grew the way the seed packet promised.

Her mother at the sink, sleeves rolled up, humming—always humming—under her breath when storms rattled the windows. The tune had never had proper words. Sometimes it picked one up for a verse or two; sometimes it was just nonsense, vowel sounds wrapped around breathy consonants.

Safe in. Rain out. Kettle on. Walls hold.

Mara swallowed.

Liora glanced at her, a flick in her gaze that said, It’s your turn.

Mara opened her mouth.

The first sound stuck.

Her throat, traitorous, went dry. Her tongue felt thick. For a heartbeat, all she could think of was the circle of faces, the weight of the entire city’s attention, the absurdity of singing a kitchen-lullaby into an ancient, humming machine in a place no one she knew above would believe existed.

She almost stopped.

Then she remembered the Whale’s lanterns, scarred and uneven. The thin crack of light that had crept up Finn’s graph. The way Nerissa had said, I want your eyes. Because they haven’t learned yet how to look away.

She thought of her mother’s hand on her back during thunder, smoothed in time with that silly little song, and of how storms had seemed less huge when her fear had something small and human-sized to hang on to.

She sang.

Her voice was thinner than Liora’s, higher, with that slight rawness of a child’s voice not yet settled. It wobbled on the first note, then found its footing.

She didn’t think about how it sounded. She thought about her kitchen table. About rain on glass. About the way light flickered on wet pavement.

“Tip-tap on the window, rain on the glass,” she sang into the humming air. “Boil up the kettle, let the dark clouds pass…”

The words felt strange here, where there were no windows in the surface sense, no kettles (unless you counted Finn’s complaining contraption), no sky to see clouds through. Strange—but not wrong.

She kept going.

Roof over, floor under, walls standing fast,  
Storm in the doorway, you can’t come in last.

Her melody braided around Liora’s.

At first, they jostled.

The two tunes were not written to sit together. One had grown from underwater lungs and the push and pull of tides; the other from radiators and the thrum of rain on thin glass. Their rhythms didn’t match; their intervals tugged at each other.

Then Liora, ears sharp, adjusted.

She bent her melody, just a little, sliding one held note down by a half-step to meet Mara’s rising one. Mara, hearing that, changed the length of her next phrase, shortening it to leave Liora’s line room to unfold under it. They wove.

Two currents, different temperatures, meeting.

The Engine’s hum shifted again.

It was more noticeable this time, enough that the nearest technicians glanced up from their valves in instinctive alarm. Finn flapped his hands at them—keep going, don’t panic—and they turned back, hands trembling only a little more as they adjusted flow at his shouted numbers.

Above, outside the dome, the Whale sang.

His call rolled down through the water, through the Veil, through the bubble of air. It hit the plaza floor like a slow earthquake, fine grains of sand lifting in tiny jumps before settling again. The coral pillars around the rim vibrated visibly.

It wasn’t a song in the sense of tidy, surface tunes with verses and a chorus. It was more like a paragraph you felt in your bones.

It was old. It tasted of ice ages and drowned forests. It moved in great, slow arcs, some notes so low Mara felt them in her marrow before she heard them as sound. No translation net tried to turn it into words this time. It was too broad for that.

The Engine took that deep note and wrapped it around Liora’s and Mara’s thinner ones.

Its membranes of suspended water shuddered, then began to pulse in time. The light threads within them swung out of their previous rigid pattern, re-mapping themselves along new spirals. Tiny, suppressed flares of stormlight that had been building at certain junctions found new routes, oozing sideways into looping channels instead of jumping straight down.

“Heartstones!” Finn cried, voice cracking. “Now!”

Around the plaza, the builders moved.

They broke from their ring, hurrying off down side corridors and into the city’s underpassages, long rods over their shoulders. Mara knew, from Finn’s frantic, chalk-scribbled map in the workshop, where they were going: key points in Pelagoria’s foundation. The cores of the biggest coral towers. The deep, fused nodes under the palace. The original stone in the first-built dome.

Each Heartstone was a thick lump of dense coral and mineral, grown and carefully hardened where loads met: silent, steady recorders of strain and rhythm. Today, they were about to become speakers.

Technicians fed new lines—thin strands of shell-wire and listening tubes—from the Engine’s base into those same conduits, linking the beating core of the machine to the city’s bones.

Liora’s song lifted. Mara’s circled under it. The Whale’s thrummed through both.

And then, from the circles, other sounds joined.

A woman near the front of the second ring, hands calloused from years of knotting nets, began to hum: low, tuneless at first, then resolving into the lilting cadence of an old work chant.

A child, clutching a parent’s hand, started tapping their feet on the fused-sand floor in a steady, two-beat rhythm. Another picked it up, then another, until a soft, syncopated patter underpinned everything, like distant, controlled rain.

An elder with a voice like cracked shell launched, without apparent warning, into a tide-call: the deep, repetitive phrases used to persuade stubborn cross-currents to shift during construction. His neighbours, after a moment’s startled hesitation, joined in, their voices rolling along the ring, imperfect but united.

The sound rose.

It wasn’t beautiful in any tidy, choir-trained way. It was too layered, too full of individual quirks. Some people were out of tune. Some couldn’t hold a pitch if it had handles. Some only hummed, others chanted words Mara didn’t know. A few clapped, hands smacking against scaled arms in off-beat emphasis.

And yet, as more voices came in, the whole became something… right.

The plaza floor vibrated. The coral pillars thrummed. Dust flaked gently from old cracks as new patterns of vibration slid into them.

Deep in the city’s foundations, the Heartstones woke.

At first, they shuddered, disconcerted. They were used to being receptors, not transmitters. The steady, ever-present tremor of the sea’s weight, the occasional sharp jab of impact, the slow drag of stress moving through rock—that was their familiar song.

Now, through the rods pressed against them by the builders’ sweating hands, a different hum came. Layered. Complex. Full of human-scale patterns.

The stones took it in.

A note from the Engine’s reshaped hum. A line from the Whale’s song. A fragment of Liora’s harbour-melody. A bar of Mara’s rain-lullaby. A hundred, a thousand small, imperfect human vibrations.

Then, obedient to the purpose they’d been grown for—carry movement, report strain—they pushed it outward.

Up through coral, down into old rock, sideways along the curved base of the dome. Into the legs of tables and the frames of doors, into the stone steps of staircases and the ribs of arches. Into the floors where children’s bare feet felt it and laughed, and where elders’ chairs rocked gently with it.

“It’s in,” Finn whispered, eyes bright, hands hovering over the Engine’s main control levers like a conductor waiting for the right moment to bring in the brass. “Come on. Come on, you stubborn beauty. Take it.”

The Engine shuddered.

For a second, it did entirely the wrong thing.

Its internal light spiked, not sideways but straight up. The membranes of held water flashed near-white, every gear’s teeth lit as if backlit by lightning. The hum jumped a whole octave, becoming a thin, keening whine that set Mara’s teeth on edge.

Several of the nearest technicians flinched, hands jerking back from hot valves. A few of the children in the outer rings clapped their hands over their ears, faces screwing up.

The Whale’s lanterns, visible through the dome above, flared sharply, as if in pain.

“No,” Liora gasped, her song stumbling.

Mara’s voice thinned.

The Engine whined higher. One of the membranes bulged outward in a sudden, alarming swell, its surface crawling with froth like a panicked wave trapped in glass.

“Finn!” Tideglass barked. “Shut it down!”

“Wait,” Finn said hoarsely, every muscle in his body taut. “It’s… overshooting. It’s… looking for the pattern. If I cut it now, it will snap back too hard.”

The plaza’s sound faltered.

People’s voices hitched, caught between instinctive silence—be quiet, something is about to break—and the knowledge that the whole point of this was not to cower.

Mara’s throat felt like she’d swallowed a handful of dry sand. She forced out the next line of her lullaby anyway, voice cracking on the second syllable.

“Storm on the doorstep, you can’t come in last…”

Her mother had always made a joke of that line. Had pulled a face at the door, as if scolding a wet dog. Mara tried to put that into the sound: the faint, ridiculous defiance of trying to boss around something much bigger than you for the sake of making your child laugh.

Liora caught it.

Her own melody, which had almost dropped away, swelled again—not louder, but… deeper. She reached back into that place where songs and promises touched and pushed more of herself into it.

“Harbour hidden, harbour home,” she sang, voice rough now. “We are not your nets to close.”

The Whale answered.

His next call rolled down like a boulder. It started low, then stepped up—not in the smooth, sliding way of before, but in rough, deliberate intervals. Three pulses. A pause. Two more. He was… counting, Mara realised dimly. Marking out beats for them.

The Engine hit its peak.

Light poured through its membranes, bright enough that Mara had to squint. For a terrifying heartbeat, it seemed every element was going to tear free: water-sheets bursting, gears spinning out of alignment, coral cracking.

Then, with a convulsive shudder that ran from its base all the way up through its listening-shell crown, it… let go.

The hard, piercing whine broke apart.

In its place, a lower, more complex chord bloomed. It wasn’t one note. It was many, layered: a deep, steady vibration that owed as much to rock as to machinery; a mid-range pulse with a rhythm suspiciously like the tide-call the elders were chanting; a higher, wavering line that bore an unmistakable echo of Liora’s melody and Mara’s kitchen-song tangled together.

The membranes of water relaxed.

They didn’t go flat—they rippled. The bulged one drew back, its surface smoothing like a frightened creature being coaxed down from a panic. Light no longer jagged through them in harsh, straight paths. Instead, it moved in spirals, in looping curls, in little branching filaments.

Those filaments shot outward.

Not in spikes, not in single, brittle lines, but in a network. They flowed along the shell-wires to the Heartstones, into the coral, into the arching bones of the dome. They pushed outward, through, around.

Up above, along the inside of the Veil, the change became visible.

Before, during storms, the shield’s reaction had been harsh: sudden flares where lightning hit, narrow streaks of over-brightness racing in straight lines across its surface, small, tight points of pressure where water slammed.

Now, when the next wave thudded against the dome, the light that marked the impact spread more evenly. Instead of a single sharp flare, a broad, softer glow rippled out in rings from the point of contact. The dome bulged inward slightly—yielding, not cracking. The next lightning stroke’s energy streaked along gentle curves instead of jagged paths, bleeding into wider arcs that faded rather than piling into stress points.

Mara felt the pressure that had been building behind her eyes ease, just a fraction, like a headache finally beginning to unclench.

The plaza heard it too.

Their humming changed—not to something new, but to something less strained. Where their voices had been tight, forced up in defiance, they loosened. Some people even smiled, incredulously, as the vibration running up through their feet and into their ribs shifted from anxiety’s brittle buzz to a steadier, warmer hum.

Liora’s song settled into the new chord like a bird folding into a new, better-fitting nest. Mara found, to her own astonishment, that her voice had stopped wobbling. She let it slip into harmony—third above here, unison there, guessing and adjusting in clumsy, earnest stabs.

The Whale’s lanterns along his flank brightened—not in that alarm-spike way, but in a gentler, more diffuse glow, like embers being breathed on.

He called again.

This time, there was no edge of flinch in it. The chord rolled through the city, through Mara’s bones, through the Engine, and back out into the Veil. The folded storm above snarled, but its teeth met something that did not shatter.

For a few moments that felt stretched-out and thin, like the surface of the tidepool Door just before it had grabbed her, Mara existed in two places at once.

Part of her was here: on the damp, slightly sticky step of the plaza platform, surrounded by a ring of people whose lives depended on a hum she was helping to shape, lungs working hard in the thin air, throat sore, ears full of new chords.

Part of her was on a grey, rocky shore, salt wind slapping her cheeks, hair whipping her eyes, the taste of rain sharp on her tongue. Captain Thimble’s battered museum door behind her, its peeling green paint under her hand. The sky overhead a roiling, bruised mass, the line of waves advancing and retreating with familiar, terrifying regularity.

It was as if a thin, bright thread ran from her chest in Pelagoria up through the dark water, through the Veil and the storm and the tidepool Door, up into the air above, anchoring her to both.

She belonged to neither, completely.

Pelagoria’s air was too thin, its sound too underwater. The surface was too loud, too dry, its people deaf to the deep. In both places she was the odd one out, the child in the wrong kind of clothes, the wrong kind of expectations.

But here, in this moment—under a dome that was learning to breathe differently, above a trench that held its ancient secrets close, between a Queen who had chosen to loosen her fear and a Whale who had chosen to sing with them anyway—her in-between-ness felt less like a mistake and more like… a bridge.

A tightrope, yes. But one she had chosen, now, to walk.

The hum reached a new equilibrium.

Professor Finn, who had been gripping the Engine’s main valves so hard his knuckles had gone white, cautiously eased off. The gauges on his panels—little shells and liquid columns and wiggling needles—stopped jerking and settled into smoother oscillations. The misbehaving kettle at his elbow sighed and produced, for the first time in its existence, a perfectly reasonable puff of steam.

Outside, above the dome, the storm did not vanish. The sky was still heavy with dark cloud. Wind still whipped the sea’s surface; waves still pounded. But some edge had gone out of it, or else the way that edge translated down here had changed.

Lightning still flashed—but when it hit the Veil now, it spread, glowed, bled sideways, instead of punching straight down. The water pressed—but the dome yielded in gentle, measurable bulges, like a lung drawing breath, then eased back.

The almost-constant, low-level crackle Mara had felt under her skin for days now quieted.

Liora’s song wound to its end, the last phrase hanging in the air like a carried question. Mara’s melody landed with it, the two notes not identical but… agreeing.

The Whale’s final chord shivered through them.

Then, slowly, carefully, the sounds in the plaza faded.

Not to silence—Pelagoria, even hushed, was never entirely silent—but to a softer background murmur. Someone’s voice cracked on a last held note and broke into a startled laugh. A child hiccuped, overwhelmed. Someone else let out a sound that wasn’t quite a sob and wasn’t quite a sigh.

Queen Nerissa’s hand slid from the Engine’s rib.

Her shoulders, which had been held in a tension so tight Mara’s own back ached in sympathy, dropped by about half an inch.

“It holds,” Finn whispered, as much to himself as to anyone. “It… actually holds.”

He looked incredulous, delighted, terrified in advance of discovering what, exactly, they had just done.

Nerissa turned back to the crowd.

Her eyes were shining. Whether with tears or with reflected stormlight, Mara couldn’t say.

“The Veil has… changed,” Nerissa said. “You felt it. You made it. It is no longer a wall we lean against, hoping it does not crack. It is a cloak we must wear, and move with.”

She stepped closer to the plaza’s edge, voice gentler now.

“It will not stop all storms,” she said. “It will not make hooks vanish or greed evaporate. It will make some things… easier. Others… harder. We will feel more of Madam’s moods. We will hear more of the Surface’s noise. In return, we will hurt the deep less. The Whale will sing without flinching. The currents will have more of their own say. We will be… less alone in our bubble.”

She glanced up, through the dome, at the drifting lights of the Whale.

Above, one of his lanterns near his great head flared and dimmed in a slow, deliberate pattern. A greeting. An acknowledgment. A thank you, as much as someone that ancient and that proud ever gave.

Nerissa’s mouth curved, just for a heartbeat.

“Today,” she said, “we chose not to squeeze our fear tighter. We chose to widen, instead. Thank you.”

The thank you wasn’t just for the Council, or for Finn, or even for Liora and Mara. It was for the whole messy, humming mass of them: for the woman with the work-rough hands who’d hummed by reflex, for the child whose off-beat foot taps had given Finn a new timing idea, for the old man whose out-of-tune tide-call had nevertheless slotted into the Engine’s need for a particular low-frequency pulse.

For a city that had, despite everything, decided to trust a plan that wasn’t sensible.

Around the plaza, people began to exhale properly.

Someone cheered, abruptly, half in disbelief. It broke whatever last, ritualistic stillness had been holding. The circles rippled. Laughter broke out in little pockets, nervous and bright. A few people hugged whoever was nearest, regardless of prior acquaintance. Others sagged against pillars, hands pressed to chests, feeling their own heartbeats and the new, deeper hum beneath them.

Children who’d been kept still under stern parental hands for the duration burst free, racing onto the plaza floor, skidding and whooping in the residual vibrations. One of them, a little boy with a shock of hair that floated like an escapee anemone, ran up to Mara, skidded to a halt, and blurted, “Your song was wrong.”

Mara blinked. “Thank you?”

“In a good way,” he said earnestly. “It went… up when it should go… down. It tickled.”

He dashed off again before she could answer.

Liora let out a breath that turned into a laugh halfway through.

“That was—” She flapped a hand, at a rare loss. “—a lot.”

Mara nodded, throat too tight to say much. Her legs were shaking. Now that the moment had passed, her body had decided to remember it existed.

“You did it,” Finn said, voice hoarse. “Both of you. All of you.” He spread his arms to take in the plaza, the city, the invisible currents beyond. “Madam only knows what we’ve set in motion.”

“Something,” Liora said. “Better than… nothing.”

Mara looked up through the softened light of the dome.

Beyond it, far above, she could just make out, in her mind if not entirely with her eyes, the bruise-coloured sky over her town. The storm there would still be heavy. The rain would still batter the windows. The sea would still hurl itself at the seawall.

But perhaps—if the Veil was humming differently now, letting some energy through instead of bottling it all in one place, syncing a little better with the Whale’s song—the edges of that storm would be softer. The waves a fraction less vicious. The high-tide line a little less keen on climbing concrete steps.

No one up there would know why.

They’d talk about wind-shear, perhaps, or shifting pressure systems. Some might attribute it to luck. Others wouldn’t notice at all, because the worst hadn’t happened, and disasters averted rarely made the news.

Mara would know.

She would stand at her bedroom window, watching clouds drag themselves away, and feel the echo of this hum in her chest. She would walk down to the tidepool when the sea pulled back and see if the Door-arch still glowed, still tugged. She would go to Kelp & Curiosities, push open that stubborn green door, and tell Captain Thimble Kelp that the sea had opinions about his guilt.

On this side, she would come back to a city that hummed with a new, flexible shield. To a Queen learning to live with a cloak instead of a wall. To a Whale with fewer flinches in his song. To a friend who knew, viscerally, what it was to straddle two kinds of breath.

Between storm and deep, between hum and song, Mara Bright hung for a moment like one of the Engine’s suspended water-sheets: stretched thin, shimmering, held in an impossible balance.

Then someone in the crowd tripped and knocked into someone else, a child squealed, a technician yelped as Finn’s kettle finally boiled over, and the moment—delicate, perfect, terrifying—folded itself up and joined the long, complicated chord of the day.

The city breathed.

The sea, above and below, adjusted its grip.

And the new shield—not a wall, not a prison, but a woven, living thing humming with stories and songs from both worlds—settled itself around Pelagoria like a cloak that, for the first time in a long time, knew how to move with the tides.
