# Storms Above, Cracks Below

Where a gathering storm on the surface mirrors the growing instability of Pelagoria’s shield, forcing Queen Nerissa to face old fears and long-buried promises.

The bubble-boat hit the turbulence like a toy in a washing machine.

One moment, they were rising in a relatively dignified manner, Pelagoria’s hazy glow expanding above them, the dark weight of the trench receding below. The next, the water around them clenched.

A sideways shove grabbed the submersible and flung it half a body-length to the left. Mara’s shoulder slammed into Liora’s; Liora’s elbow smacked into the central post; Professor Finn, who had not yet invented a safety harness he trusted, bounced off the interior of the bubble with a muffled “oof.”

Outside, the water boiled.

It wasn’t the usual, stately shift of a current deep below. This was choppier, less… polite. Dancey little whirlpools snapped into being around them, their edges marked by furious bands of bubbles. The light from Pelagoria’s distant dome lurched up and down as if on loose strings.

“Eeep,” Bubbles said, in a tone that did not inspire confidence. “No. No, thank you. I do not like this. I did not sign up for this.”

“You didn’t sign up for anything, you were assembled,” Finn grunted, grabbing a lever and shoving it hard to the right. “Adjusting pitch. Hold on. Don’t—”

The sub lurched again, this time up and back. Something thudded against the outer shell—a rock? A startled fish with poor reflexes?—and rattled the metal ring around them.

Glimmers of light flickered at the edge of Mara’s vision—not from above, but from the side, where there shouldn’t have been anything but darkness and the occasional, respectful glimmer of jellyfish.

“What is that?” she gasped.

Liora, one hand clamped white-knuckled around a pipe, twisted to look. Her pupils, already dilated in the low light, widened further.

“The Veil,” she whispered. “It’s… showing.”

Mara pressed her forehead against the inner wall of the bubble, squinting through the churning water.

For a heartbeat, the space around Pelagoria was no longer invisible.

An enormous, shimmering film bulged through the murk: the outline of the city’s protective shell, usually intangible as temperature, now lit up like a soap bubble catching a flash. It encased Pelagoria in a huge, imperfect sphere, its surface striated with lines of force that reminded Mara disturbingly of the Whispering Map in Liora’s library—currents drawn in frantic, overlapping loops.

Where the storm-warped water above hammered down, the shield thickened visibly, flaring with pale, blue-white light. Lightning from the surface—thin, vicious jabs of brilliance—stabbed into that light and jerked sideways, running along the bubble’s outside like spilled electricity along a wire.

The whole immense dome quivered.

“Professor…” Mara began.

“I see it,” Finn said grimly. He twisted three valves in quick succession. The engine’s hum—normally so deep and steady Mara barely noticed it—had climbed into an angry, mid-range whine. Through the hull, she could feel tiny vibrations ratcheting up, like a kettle being boiled past boiling.

“Bubbles,” Finn said carefully, “how are your seals?”

“Very, very concerned,” Bubbles said. “The outside is pulling one way and the humming is pushing another. I feel… elongated.”

Another jolt hit them, this one more of a twist than a shove. The sub rolled thirty degrees. Liora slipped, catching herself at the last second on a protruding brace. Mara’s backpack, for the first time in its life, achieved a sort of sideways flight, smacking Finn in the hip.

“Up,” Finn muttered. “We need to get inside that mess before it decides we don’t belong.”

The fan-fins strained, flared, beat harder. The sub nosed upward like a very determined bubble in fizzy drink.

Through the chaos, the city grew: the smudge of Pelagoria’s lights resolving into individual domes, bridges, towers. Along the outermost curve, Mara could see faint cracks of brightness racing like lightning along the line where the protective membrane met the rock. Pieces of the city’s architecture itself—the outer coral ridges, the highest walkways—glowed at the edges, thin and strained.

The storm over the surface—that far, unseen drama—was pounding its opinions straight down through miles of water, and the Veil, instead of softening, was… grabbing.

“It’s amplifying,” Finn said under his breath. “Of course it is. Stupid, beautiful machine. It’s pushing back so hard it’s singing the storm louder.”

“The Whale tried to tell us,” Liora said through her teeth. “The hum’s wrong.”

“Can we get through?” Mara asked. “The shield—”

As if in answer, a section of the shimmering bubble near them shimmered more violently, its surface thickening into a milky film. For a split second, Mara thought they were going to slam straight into it.

At the last instant, the film… parted.

The bubble-boat shot through a brief, circular opening, like a fish darting through a net’s torn ring. Light bent oddly around the edges; Mara’s stomach swooped again, that Door-feeling back for a heartbeat.

Then they were inside.

The churning at least changed flavour. The water between the Veil and the city was still rough—shaken by the storm pounding above—but it no longer tried to twist them in three directions at once. The fan-fins relaxed by a fractional degree. Bubbles’ voice dropped half an octave from sheer, shaking relief.

Inside the dome, Pelagoria was in motion.

The graceful loops of traffic Mara had seen before—people drifting along air-bridges, fish weaving through water-tunnels, small maintenance submersibles trundling about their business—had dissolved into something closer to purposeful chaos.

Lanterns along streets and in domes flickered as the power grid, such as it was, tried to cope with the Veil’s tantrum. Alarm-shells hung from arches shrieked in long, sobbing tones, their sound echoing weirdly through the water and air. From where their sub approached one of the lower harbours, Mara could see small figures—Pelagorians—racing along walkways, some diving in coordinated groups into water-tunnels, others shepherding clusters of children into deeper buildings.

“They’re heading for the inner vaults,” Liora said, voice tight. “Storm-shelters. For if the dome… breaks.”

Mara swallowed. “Has that… ever happened?”

“Not entirely,” Liora said. “Not here. Not yet.”

The engines whined as Finn coaxed Bubbles toward the harbour intake.

Inside the palace, the alarms sounded different.

They were deeper there, each slow toll of the massive warning-shells reverberating along the coral ribs of the High Hall and down into the foundations. Queen Nerissa stood at the very top of the palace’s central spire, just below the highest bulge of the dome, her hands braced on a rail grown from coral, watching the sky above the sea boil.

From here, the storm felt almost close enough to touch.

Waves, swelled and sharpened by wind, hurled themselves against the invisible barrier. Each impact sent a shudder down through Pelagoria’s shell, visible from the inside as a brief, bright flare along the curved ceiling. Sometimes the Veil yielded a little, dimpling inward, then pushing back out with a rebound that made the water within heave.

Lightning stitched the dark sky beyond, line after jagged line splitting cloud and air. Every time it struck, the point of impact on the Veil flared like a camera flash reflected in glass, scattering spiderwebs of light along the entire surface.

For years, the shield had been the city’s quiet, unseen blessing. Its presence felt only by those who listened, its workings known in detail to only a handful of engineers and a Queen who had signed the old agreements with salt on her fingers.

Now, under the assault of a storm that would, once, have been simply part of the sea’s temper, it was visible. Obvious. A bubble in the middle of the deep, filled with stolen air and stubborn fear, outlined again and again by the sky’s anger.

Nerissa’s heart thudded each time the Veil’s surface flashed.

If any ship passed overhead now—any trawler, any drilling platform, any cruise liner packed with people taking pictures of waves—the bubble would be unmistakable. An enormous, shimmering lens in the water. The perfect, glittering target for curiosity and greed.

Old memories rose, unbidden, riding the thunder.

She smelled, suddenly, tar and pitch and the sharp, dry smell of canvas, layered over the salt. Heard laughter in a language she had once spoken fluently, shouted over the slap of waves against wooden hulls.

Stood again on the deck of an old ship, her fingers webbed for swimming and ink-stained from recording new agreements, watching a man with a ruddy, windburned face and eyes the colour of winter sky grin at her over a crate of strange, glittering trinkets.

“We trade in stories,” Captain Alder Bright had said then, his teeth bright in the lamplight. “Yours from below, ours from above. Value for value, promise for promise. No nets. No hooks. No cages. That’s the bargain.”

She’d believed him. For a time, he’d made good on it.

Then had come other captains.

Nerissa’s fingers tightened on the rail until the fine scales along their backs rasped faintly against coral.

She remembered the slap of a net breaking the surface near the first Door, years later, full not of fish but of gasping, struggling people dragged up from a flooded coastal village. Remembered pulling them free, one by one, promising temporary shelter, sanctuary, while the sea raged above.

Remembered the clutter of surface objects that had followed: more crates, more tools, more “gifts.” Plastic that didn’t rot. Shiny things that tumbled into gullies and stayed. Oil, leaked and left.

Remembered the first time a child from above had asked, voice quavering, if they could stay in Pelagoria forever, because the land felt unsafe now. Remembered finding out later that a few had not gone home at all, having slipped into depths they didn’t understand, chasing magic instead of learning to swim.

Remembered arguments in the Council Hall that had lasted days. The word contamination thrown back and forth like a weapon. The word invasion spat like a curse. The word protect whispered like a prayer.

Remembered, finally, standing where she stood now, watching another storm slam the surface above, and saying the words that had cut a whole world off.

“No more doors.”

Thimble Kelp’s ship had been the last to come that way. The last to pass freely through. The last to see Pelagoria openly.

She had watched him go, jaw clenched, heart pounding, listening as Finn’s newly-constructed Engine spun up into the soft, humming barrier that had hidden them ever since.

Now, the Engine’s hum was fighting the sea instead of dancing with it.

Alarms from below—a different tone, sharp and insistent—cut through the groan of worked water.

“Your Majesty!” A voice at her shoulder, breathless. Nerissa turned.

Tideglass, her Captain of the Guard, stood there, scales along one cheek still spattered with droplets from the hurried swim up. Their fin-crest lay flat against their skull, a sure sign of strain.

“The lower harbours report structural stress along the western arc,” Tideglass said. “Some of the older coral is… complaining. We’ve sealed three intake tunnels as a precaution. The first inner vaults are full. There’s… some panic.”

“‘Some,’” Nerissa repeated, dry.

“We’re redirecting flow away from the pressure points as best we can, but there are… odd echoes,” Tideglass went on. “The currents don’t… listen properly. Like they’re hearing two songs at once and can’t decide which to follow.”

“Finn’s Engine,” Nerissa said.

“Yes,” Tideglass said. “The hum doesn’t match the storm’s… beat.”

Another flash, another shudder. Down along the city’s outer skin, one of the visible cracks of light—thin fractures in the membrane’s usual silence—flickered wider for a terrifying instant, then narrowed. She saw tiny silhouettes darting along a nearby walkway, hurrying past.

“If we strengthen the Engine, amplify the Veil,” Tideglass said, “we might… ride it out. Make the dome harder. Less… yielding.”

“Or we might tear more cracks,” Nerissa said, “by pulling harder against the wrong rhythm.”

Below, in the palace, a chorus of voices rose—a low, anxious rumble.

“They’re waiting,” Tideglass said quietly. “For you.”

Nerissa took a breath of the thin, strained air inside the dome.

“It is what queens are for,” she murmured. “To be looked at while the walls shake.”

She turned from the storm.

The High Hall was not as she’d left it.

Pelagoria’s ruling chamber, carved into the very heart of the palace, usually held itself with a certain solemn, quiet grandeur. Coral pillars arched up into a ceiling alive with muted bioluminescence. The wide space before the throne-platform gave room for debate, for discussion.

Today, it was a tidepool of barely contained chaos.

Councilors clustered in knots, robes rustling, coral ornaments clacking faintly as they gestured with more passion than dignity. A few had abandoned the usual formal coral crowns for practical caps or head-wraps more suitable to emergency swims. The air was thick with overlapping complaints, proposals, fears.

As Nerissa stepped down toward her seat, the sound swelled, then broke over her like surf as the councilors surged toward the centre to be heard.

“—must increase power to the Engine—”

“—shut down all outer currents, treat this as a siege—”

“—this proves the Veil is failing, we must evacuate to the deep vaults—”

“—leave Pelagoria?” Someone’s voice, high, shocky.

“No one is suggesting—”

“We must shut all Doors,” another insisted. “That child from above—”

“Mara Bright,” Nerissa said, and the name cut clearly through the babble.

The hall stilled, just for a beat, as everyone remembered that she knew it. That she had spoken with the bright-eyed trespasser from the storm’s between-space.

Queen Nerissa walked the last steps to her throne without hurry, despite the storm raging above and the fear swirling below. She took her seat, the living coral subtly shifting to support her as if sensing the weight she carried.

The murmurs resumed, but lower now, as currents of protocol reclaimed some of the flow.

“Bring them in,” Nerissa said. Her voice carried easily, no need to raise it; the hall was designed to let the Queen’s words reach every corner.

The great doors at the far end swung open.

Professor Finn entered first, beard slightly askew, suit damp in patches that suggested he’d forgotten to dry thoroughly after disembarking. He looked more rattled than Mara had ever seen him, but there was a set to his jaw that said he’d grown tired of apologising only in his head.

Liora walked beside him.

Her tunic clung damply. Her hair, still faintly buoyant from the thinner air, drifted around her shoulders. Lines of tension bracketed her mouth, but her chin was high, her eyes steady. The jellyish smudge of worry on her brow had been joined by a harder, sharper resolve.

Mara came just behind them, tiny compared to the arching coral and towering councilors, her trainers leaving faint wet marks on the stone.

The chamber’s reaction to her was audible.

A hiss here, sucked in between teeth. A mutter there. The word surfacer threaded through the whispers in a dozen worried intonations: accusation, fear, grim satisfaction that their warnings about surface entanglements had been right all along.

“Silence,” Nerissa said, not loud, and the word dropped like a stone into the hall’s water, sending stillness rippling out.

Finn bowed with a kind of lopsided dignity, as if he were used to standing in front of hostile committees and had long ago decided embarrassment was a waste of time. Liora inclined her head, enough respect shown, no more. Mara hovered, unsure of the correct protocol, then settled for a small, awkward nod.

“The Lantern Whale has spoken,” Nerissa said. “You have heard him. Report.”

Finn cleared his throat, glancing once at Mara and Liora as if drawing courage from their smaller, stubborn forms.

“The Whale confirms what my instruments hinted and what my conscience has been trying to say louder for some time,” he said. “The Engine—the Harmoniser—my… machine—is in conflict with Madam’s natural rhythms. Where it should bend with the storm, it is bracing. Where it should soften, it is… sharpening.”

A murmur of alarm, indignation, I-told-you-sos washed around the chamber.

“It was meant to protect us,” Finn said over the swell, his voice sharper than usual. “To make it harder for ships to see us, for sonar to ping us back, for drills to burrow near. It did that. It did it well. But it did more. It made the currents forget some of their own paths. It sang too loud. The Whale’s lanterns bear the scars.”

A flicker, unbidden, of the glowing sores along that ancient flank passed behind Mara’s eyes.

“The storm above is worse because of us?” one councilor demanded, scandalised. “We are—what—insulting the weather now?”

“The storm would be there regardless,” Finn said. “The Surface has become… very good at stirring the sky. But our Veil—my Engine—is taking what should be a rolling blow and turning parts of it into a direct… punch.”

“Technical term?” Tideglass muttered.

“Regrettable metaphor,” Finn said. “The pressure on certain points of the dome is… amplified. That’s why you’re seeing cracks of light where you never have before.”

He gestured upwards. As if on cue, as if the sea itself appreciated good timing, a deep boom rolled through the hall. Light flashed along the dome’s curve, filtered through layers of water and shell, turning the ceiling briefly into a flickering second ocean.

“And the Door?” an old councilor with a particularly elaborate collar said. His voice shook more with outrage than age. “The surfacer-girl’s fall? Also this Engine’s fault?”

Mara colour-spiked—from shame, from anger.

“The Door opened because the hum pressed too hard on Madam’s patience,” Finn said. “And because the Queen’s fear—that we would be found, invaded, hurt—was woven tight into the workings. We pulled too hard on the sea’s fabric. It… pulled back. Finding the old weak spots. The old… promises.”

He looked at Nerissa as he said that last word.

In her throne, Nerissa’s fingers tightened imperceptibly. Old promises. Made in storms like this, with hands that had held ropes worn by generations of sailors and fins that had traced the edges of maps now buried under decisions.

“And what did the Whale say?” Tideglass asked. Their voice was less accusing, more tautly curious. “Straight, Finn. No… metaphors.”

“He said the Veil is fear made into current,” Liora said, stepping forward before Finn could soften it. Her voice shook, but with outrage, not nerves. “He said our cloak is too tight. That we are choking his songs and the sea’s. That hiding has a cost the deep is paying for us.”

Gasps. One of the councilors half-rose, then sat again as if the floor had moved.

“He said,” Mara added, not sure if it was allowed for her to address an entire underwater government but deciding this was not the time to worry about etiquette, “that fear that doesn’t listen is as dangerous as greed that doesn’t share.”

A beat of stunned silence followed. The translation net, bless it, delivered her mangled translation of whale-poetry with a strange, solemn weight.

“And that,” one councilor said acidly, “is exactly the sort of thing a child would find profound.”

Mara’s heart hammered. She was about to mumble something defensive when Nerissa spoke.

“And what would you call it?” she said, eyes on the councilor. “Comfortable?”

The man shut his mouth.

Mara’s spine uncurled a fraction. Liora’s shoulder bumped her own, a tiny, conspiratorial nudge.

“The Whale also said,” Finn put in, “that if we do not alter the Engine’s song, Madam Ocean will do it for us. And when she tears, she does not… mend with care.”

“Then we must strengthen it,” someone burst out—Councilor Virel, whose family had lost two cousins to a trawler net generations ago and had never let the story silt over. “More hum. More power. If the sea pushes, we push back. Better a few cracks than open rifts. Better strain than invasion.”

“And if the Engine tears the Veil wide open?” a younger councilor shot back. “If a storm like this one rends our cloak and shows us to every ship above? We sit here arguing theoreticals while the Surface drops nets straight through our ceilings.”

“We could move,” an older woman said unexpectedly. Heads turned. She stood near one of the lower arches, her coral crown askew, eyes distant. “Abandon Pelagoria. Go deeper. Away from the storms. Away from the lanes. Find a quieter hollow. There are trenches unclaimed. Grottos Madam has not yet lent to cities.”

Mur-mur- ripple. The idea spread through the hall in a low, uneasy wave.

“Leave our home?” Tideglass said, more wounded than angry. “The coral we’ve grown for generations? The maps our grandparents etched? The doors—that we sealed at such cost?”

“Would you rather be found?” the older woman snapped. “Pulled out by hooks, your air stolen, your children gawped at behind glass—”

“You speak as if that is already happening,” someone else cut in.

“It happened,” the woman retorted. “Once. Twice. How many times do we test their greed?”

“Enough,” Nerissa said, and the word had weight.

The hall fell quiet again. Outside, lightning stitched another jagged seam in the upper world. The boom rolled through the water a breath later, the dome’s shell shuddering minutely. The light along its inner surface flickered, lingered, then faded.

Nerissa let the silence stretch for a beat longer, long enough for the weight of the storm to press down on every mind in the room.

“We have two doors before us,” she said. “One swings into greater isolation. Strengthen the Engine. Thicken the Veil. Pull tighter on Madam’s currents until she slaps us away. We may buy ourselves years. A generation. Perhaps two. At the cost of the Whale’s song and the sea’s patience.”

She inclined her head toward the older councilor who’d suggested flight.

“The other door swings into flight,” she went on. “Abandon Pelagoria. Flee deeper. Wrap ourselves tighter in the dark. Trust that depth and distance will do what diplomacy and listening have not. Pray that the Surface never drills that far. That the old things in the trenches do not wish to… notice.”

Her gaze travelled the room. Many wouldn’t meet it. A few held firm.

“Either way,” Nerissa said softly, “we are choosing from fear. Closing our hands until we crush what we hold.”

The floor beneath Mara’s feet vibrated slightly; a hairline crack of light flickered briefly in one of the High Hall’s side walls where the living shell met older stone. A child’s muffled whimper, quickly hushed, came from a cluster of families sheltering near an inner arch.

Nerissa’s jaw worked.

“I chose fear once,” she said, more to the ceiling than the crowd. “On a night like this. After a storm like this. When ships had come too often and too hungry. When hooks had dipped into our corridors and oil had smeared our schools. I chose to close the doors. To hum louder than the sky.”

Her eyes flicked to Finn. To Liora. To Mara.

“And I would still close those particular doors,” she added. “I do not regret stopping the nets and the cages. But I regret that I stopped listening as well. That I took my fear of what the Surface might do and wove it into every current around our city. I forgot that the sea does not stop at our walls. That our safety cannot be welded on top of everyone else’s without… cost.”

She looked at Mara fully now.

“You came through Madam’s anger and the Engine’s stubbornness,” she said. “You breathed where you should have drowned. You listened where many of my own people would have shut their ears. You have seen both worlds in a way none of us has for a long time.”

Mara’s throat closed. Every eye in the chamber seemed to shift, however slightly, in her direction.

“I am ten,” she wanted to say. “I still mix up my long division. I spent an entire week last year convinced a shadow on my wall was a ghost.”

Out loud, what came instead was, “On the surface… storms are getting worse. My mum says it’s… climate change.” The translation net stumbled slightly over the unfamiliar term, rendering it in Pelagorian as “sky-water sickness.” “The sea’s… higher. Sometimes. Colder. Sometimes not. Everyone keeps saying ‘we’ll adapt.’ But we… don’t. Not really. Not yet. We just… talk about it. And build more walls. And buy more… stuff.”

She swallowed.

“When I saw the Veil from outside…” She groped for words. “It felt… like one of our walls. But made of… worry. Huge. Bright. Saying ‘keep out’ so loudly that it… hurt. Not just… up there. Here.” She put a hand on her chest. “And the Whale… he’s… caught in it. Between our fear and their… noise.”

She met Nerissa’s gaze and, because the storm had already torn so much open, said the thing no adult at home would have wanted to hear from a child.

“If we keep pretending we’re the only ones in danger,” she said, “we’re going to break everything.”

A slow intake of breath moved through the room like a small tide.

Liora stepped up beside her, shoulders brushing. “We can’t make ourselves safe by making the sea sick,” she said. “We can’t hide by tying the currents in knots. The Whale said we should soften the hum. Loosen the cloak.”

“And let ships find us?” Virel demanded, half-risen again. “Let drills pierce our domes? Let curious surfacers poke their fingers into every bubble we’ve grown?”

“Maybe some will,” Liora said bluntly. “Maybe we’ll have to push some back. But we can’t decide they’re all the same just because some of them brought nets.” Her voice cracked a little. “If we do that, we’re not better than the ones who looked at us and saw… monsters to catch.”

A few councilors flinched. Others stiffened.

“We need a third path,” Finn said quietly into the charged silence. “Not stronger hum. Not deeper hiding. Something… less simple. More tiring. Like most good solutions.”

Nerissa’s lips quirked despite the tension. “Spoken like a man whose inventions never do what they’re told,” she said.

“Madam will not let us pretend she is a wall we can simply lean against harder,” Finn said. “We must… retune. Speak with her instead of shouting over her. Turn the Engine from a barrier into… a translator.”

“And risk opening doors again,” the older councilor murmured.

“Yes,” Finn said. “Properly. Not just where they crack on their own. Controlled openings. With rules. Agreements. Songs. The way you did… before we shut everything.”

Nerissa’s mind flickered back, unbidden, to Captain Alder Bright’s earnest face. To Thimble’s younger eyes, full of stories and nervous wonder as he’d first stepped through the shimmering Door into Pelagoria’s High Hall.

To the day she’d told him not to come back.

She remembered the look in his eyes then: not just fear, not just betrayal, but hurt. The particular pain of having a door shut in your face by someone you’d thought of as a friend.

“How did that go?” someone scoffed. “Your ‘agreements.’ Our ‘diplomacy.’ Hooks, again. Poison, again.”

“We picked the wrong people,” Nerissa said, the admission scraping her throat raw. “We listened to the loudest captains. The richest traders. The ones with the biggest ships. We did not listen to children, or… quiet inventors, or… librarians with bad habits.”

Her gaze slid briefly toward Liora’s secret spoiling shelves.

“We made deals with those who wanted glitter, not balance,” she went on. “And when it went wrong, we decided all Surface was… wrong. I am tired of declarations made from hurt and fear and… preciousness. We have no more time for that.”

Thunder muttered faintly overhead, its sound thickened by water and architecture. The next strike of lightning made the Veil’s ceiling flare almost continuously for a heartbeat, outlines of coral towers thrown into stark relief against the sudden brightness.

“If we keep humming the way we have,” Nerissa said, “we will tear something that cannot be easily patched. If we fortify the Veil out of panic, we may save ourselves from being seen and doom others to storms they cannot outrun. If we flee to the trenches, we will trade one kind of risk for another, deeper and less… negotiable.”

She looked at Mara. At Liora. At Finn.

“And if we do nothing, if we let old fear and new storms grind against each other unchecked…” She spread her hands. “We leave our fate to Madam’s exhaustion.”

A low, bitter laugh from someone at the back. “Queens are for choosing when the sea refuses to be simple,” they muttered.

“Then I will choose this,” Nerissa said.

The hall tightened, waiting.

“We will weaken the Veil,” she said, and the words dropped like a stone into still water, sending shockwaves out. “Not tear it down. Not yet. But we will loosen the knots we tied in Madam’s skirts. We will retune the Engine to hum with the Whale’s song, not over it. We will let some currents through. Let some paths return.”

Cries erupted—half protest, half exclamation.

“Your Majesty—!”

“Madness—”

“Inviting—”

“Listening,” Nerissa cut across. “And in return, we will ask something of the Surface.”

A snort. “Ah yes. I’m sure they’ll be very eager to sign treaties drawn in algae.”

“They did, once,” Nerissa said. “And broke them. We broke ours too. We all carry that failure. But I would rather attempt a new promise, with new people, than drown quietly under the weight of my old ones.”

Her eyes fell to Mara again.

“Some surfacers are trying to mend their sky-water,” she said, quieter. “To fish differently. To poison less. To listen. Most of them are ignored. Or treated like children in a room full of frightened elders.” Her mouth twisted. “I know what that feels like.”

Soft, bitter agreement murmured through the hall, especially from the younger councilors.

“We will not open Doors without conditions,” Nerissa continued. “No nets. No hooks. No walls pushed outward. We will not offer ourselves as a sanctuary for those who wish to escape the consequences of their own harm while continuing to harm. But we will find people—few at first, carefully chosen—with whom to share hums. Stories. Perhaps even maps.”

A ripple of horror at that. Maps were powerful. So were shared songs.

“And who will you trust to choose these surfacers?” Virel demanded. “To stand at the Door and judge hearts? You? The man who built the humming wall? The princess who sneaks into forbidden libraries?”

“No,” Nerissa said. “I will ask those who are already walking on the edges to do it.”

Her gaze settled fully on Mara and Liora, as if drawing a line of light between them.

“A child who has seen the shoreline of both worlds,” she said. “And a child of mine who walks in both waters. They will hum where we cannot. They will see cracks we overlook. They will make mistakes,” she added, before anyone could accuse them for her. “So will we. But they will not be weighted by the same old fears. And they have already done what none of us dared: talk to the Whale and admit we were wrong.”

Mara’s heart pounded so hard she could feel it in her gums.

“I—I don’t know anything about treaties,” she blurted. “Or… diplomacy. Or… fixing an entire ocean.”

Nerissa’s mouth softened, just a fraction. “Nor did I, when I signed my first one,” she said. “I learned. Badly, at times. You will not do this alone. You will carry questions to your world. We will listen to your answers. Liora will carry ours to you. Professor Finn will make sure his machines whisper instead of shout.”

“And if the Surface doesn’t listen?” someone asked. “If they laugh at a child with salt on her shoes?”

“Then we will know,” Nerissa said. “Truly. And we will choose from truth, not old stories and old hurt. Maybe we will decide then to make the Engine a wall again. Maybe we will leave Pelagoria. But at least we will have tried a third path. Not just cowering behind the first wall we built and praying it does not crack.”

A crack of light flickered along the hall’s side, brighter this time. Tiny chips of old calcified coral dusted down into the air like very slow snow. A child coughed.

Nerissa stood.

Outside, the storm still battered the Veil, but there was a subtle difference in its rhythm now. Whether Madam had tired a little, or whether something in Finn’s recalibrations—he had, Mara realised with a start, been turning small wheels and muttering equations under his breath throughout—had already begun to tug the Engine’s song into a slightly new key, she couldn’t say.

“We do not have the luxury of time for perfect solutions,” Nerissa said. “We only have decisions. This is mine. We loosen. We listen. We trust, carefully. We ask the Sea’s forgiveness and our children’s help.”

Her gaze rested, last, on Liora.

The girl met it steadily, brown eyes ringed with the faintest hint of salt-irritation from deep-water pressure and deeper conversations.

“You will have to go up,” Nerissa said, and the words cost her. “Properly. Through a Door we choose. Not tumbling through a storm’s tantrum. You will breathe their air. See their sky. Judge their… hums for yourself.”

Liora’s throat worked. For a heartbeat, Mara saw the flicker of a younger Liora in her face: a child staring up at the shimmering distortion of the surface from just under the waves, wanting and fearing in equal measure.

“Yes,” Liora said. “I will.”

“And you,” Nerissa said to Mara. “Will come back. Not just to visit. To carry what you see to us. To be answerable to both. That is… a harsh ask of a child. If you say no, I will not blame you.”

Mara’s mind flashed, for a moment, to her kitchen table. To cereal. To homework. To her mother’s anxious texts. To Captain Thimble’s cluttered museum, its walls full of ghosts and its owner full of regrets.

To the Whale’s great eye, full of patient ache.

To the storm thundering above them, gnawing at the edges of the bubble they all sat in.

“If I say no,” she said slowly, “I’ll still know. About… all this. I’ll still see… storms and rubbish and fish where they shouldn’t be and… cracks.” She swallowed. “And I won’t be able to… not… do anything.”

She took a breath, thin but steady.

“So… yes,” she said. “I’ll… try. To go back and forth. To… tell you what I see. To tell them what you say. Even if they… don’t listen. Even if I’m… just a kid with sandy shoes and weird friends.”

Liora’s hand found hers and squeezed, hard enough to hurt.

“And I,” Liora said, “will… annoy whoever needs annoying on this side. Including you, Mother.”

Nerissa almost smiled. “That,” she said, “I never doubted.”

The hall breathed.

Outside, a particularly savage wave crashed against the Veil. Light flared all along the dome. For a moment, the shield’s shape was perfectly visible from within: a vast, perfect sphere of trembling brilliance, encasing their city like a drop of air in an oceanic throat.

Then—instead of rebounding harshly as before—the shimmer seemed to… shiver. The flaring smoothed, just a fraction. The afterglow lingered in a softer pattern.

Finn stared up, his engineer’s mind already breaking the sight into pulses and diagrams.

“Did you…?” Mara began.

“I… tweaked,” he admitted. “Ever so slightly. Turned a few knobs in the direction of… listening, rather than resisting. If Madam approves, she may let the Engine sing a little less off-key.”

As if in answer, a different sound rolled faintly through the hall.

Not thunder. Not alarms.

A long, low chord, deeper than any instrument Pelagoria owned, vibrated along the dome’s ribs: the Lantern Whale, far below, testing the new shape of the Veil’s hum.

The note wobbled, just a little. Then steadied. It didn’t match, not yet. But it grated less.

Mara felt it in her bones.

Nerissa closed her eyes for a heartbeat.

“When the storm passes,” she said, opening them again, “we will count the cracks. We will mend what we can. Professor Finn will dismantle half his Engine and rebuild it with more humility.” A small, pained noise from Finn. “Liora will begin preparing an expedition to the surface. With safeguards. With songs. Not in secret this time.”

Her gaze fell, one last time, on Mara.

“And you,” she said. “Will go up when the Door is ready. Tell your people that hiding from them broke more than they know. Ask them what they are willing to… loosen.”

Mara pictured herself standing on the wet stones outside Kelp & Curiosities, rain plastering her hair to her forehead, Captain Thimble’s sign creaking overhead. Pictured walking into a town meeting, or a classroom, or a living room, and saying, The sea is angry and it’s our fault and also there’s a whole city down there. Pictured the faces. The laughter. The disbelief. The fear.

Then she pictured doing none of that. Letting the storm cut new paths every year. Watching the Veil crack a little more each season until one day it didn’t hold at all.

“I’ll… start with one person,” she said. “Someone who… already hears… weird things. And go from there.”

Liora’s lips quirked. “Professor Finn’s right,” she murmured. “You’re terrifying. In a good way.”

The storm above continued its tantrum.

But somewhere between its fury and the strained patience of the deep, something had shifted.

The Veil no longer thought only of being a wall. The Engine no longer insisted on shouting. The Queen no longer clung quite as hard to the first, tight choice she’d made in fear. And two children—one with ink on her fingers and scales on her skin, the other with salt in her shoes and questions in her throat—stood together in the place between, feeling the cracks and plotting, in their stubborn, hopeful way, how to turn them into doors.

Outside, lightning flared again.

For a moment, high above, any ship that happened to pass would have seen it: an enormous, shimmering bubble in the deep, flickering like a lantern struggling in the wind.

Then the light softened, blurred, and the bubble’s outline blurred back into water.

Not hidden.

Not yet exposed.

Poised on the brink of something new.
