# The Plan That Isn’t Sensible (and Therefore Might Work)

Where Mara, Liora, and Professor Finn hatch a risky plan to retune the shield-engine using stories, songs, and surface knowledge instead of more power.

Professor Finn’s workshop had a particular way of reacting to bad weather.

On calm days, its nets of tools and half-finished contraptions swayed gently, their shadows rocking on the walls like quiet thoughts. The bubbles that drifted up from floor vents glowed politely. The big engine in the corner hummed at a contented, background sort of pitch.

Today, with rain hammering the surface far above and the whole of Pelagoria wrapped in that strange, storm-tinged half-light, the workshop fidgeted.

The nets creaked. Spoons clinked against beakers without being touched. Bubbles drifted in from nowhere and burst against shelves with faint, disgruntled pops. The Veil engine’s hum wobbled occasionally, like a singer testing different notes under their breath.

At the centre workbench, Mara hunched over a sheet of algae-paper, pencil gripped so tightly her knuckles had gone pale.

The paper smelled faintly of brine and old leaves. Its surface had a slight give, a texture her pencil caught on in tiny, satisfying snags. She had already filled most of it with loops and lines: a tangle of curves that crisscrossed in rough circles, arrows slipping between them.

It had started, sensibly enough, as a diagram.

Now it looked more like the inside of a seashell if seashells could have nervous breakdowns.

“I keep drawing walls,” she muttered, half to herself. “Even when I’m trying not to.”

Liora lay on her stomach on the floor nearby, propped up on her elbows, legs raised behind her. In the thin air of the workshop, the fins along her ankles drifted lazily. She tapped the tip of her tail—really just the last, more flexible bit of her spine, edged with delicate fin—against the floor in a slow, repeating rhythm.

Thump, pause, thump-thump, longer pause. Thump.

“I’m not hearing walls,” she said. “I’m hearing… roofs. Over our heads. Too low.”

Thump, thump.

Professor Barnaby Finn, across the room, was trying to make tea.

This would have been fine, except that the device he was attempting to boil water in also happened to be a pressure gauge.

It looked like a copper kettle someone had crossbred with a barometer and then entangled in a nest of brass coils. A narrow glass tube ran up one side, filled with a pale, viscous liquid that rose and fell according to some principle only Finn trusted. Thin wires sprouted from its base and vanished into a tangle of instruments.

Whenever the conversation veered toward anything even slightly dangerous, the kettle let out an anxious little squeal of steam.

At their last mention of “loosening the shield,” it had shrieked like a panicked beetle and tried to climb off the stove.

Finn caught it deftly, set it back down, and patted it with one gloved hand. “None of that,” he said. “We’re all anxious. You are not special.”

The kettle burbled resentfully but stayed.

Mara chewed on the end of her pencil, studying her messy, looping lines.

“On the surface,” she said, slowing her words until they came out in something like order, “we build sea walls.”

Liora craned her head to look. “Those are literal walls?” she asked. “Not yet another metaphor?”

“Literal,” Mara said. “Concrete. Big blocks. They stick out into the sea or run along the shore. To stop waves from eating the land. To keep towns from flooding. Or to keep stupidly expensive houses from… falling in.”

Liora resumed her tail-thumping, thoughtful. “Do they work?”

“Sometimes,” Mara said. “Sort of. For a while. But the waves don’t stop. They just bounce off. Or go around. Or find somewhere else to hit harder. So… we move the problem. We don’t… solve it.”

She jabbed the paper with her pencil. “I keep thinking of the Veil like that. A bigger and bigger wall. But the Whale said that just… makes the waves... weird.”

Finn, who had been fussing with a valve, nodded absently. “Reflected energy,” he said. “You shove the force away, it comes back at you from another angle. Hydrodynamics. Politics. Same problem. Different lecture.”

“So maybe,” Liora said slowly, “we stop trying to block everything. And… start trying to… shape it.”

She rolled onto her back, staring up at the workshop’s domed ceiling, scales along her cheekbone catching stray workshop light.

“It’s like… current etiquette,” she went on. “Those awful classes. When a strong current comes through a corridor, you don’t just slam a gate in its face. You guide it. You redirect. You give it somewhere to go that won’t knock over all the children.”

“You do slam gates sometimes,” Finn said. “When those children have snuck unsupervised into the turbine galleries.”

“Hypothetically,” Liora said, with the reflex of someone who had, in fact, done exactly that. “But mostly you… bend. You don’t just… stop.”

Mara frowned at her drawing. She began sketching again, not the straight, thick line she’d used before to show the Veil’s edge, but a series of looping, overlapping arcs.

“Like… curtains,” she said slowly. “Not a brick wall. Lots of… layers. They move. Air can get through, but not… everything. And they can… billow when the wind hits, instead of… cracking.”

Finn, curiosity piqued by the absence of squealing from the kettle, wandered over to peer at her paper.

“What’s that?” he asked.

“The Veil,” Mara said. “If it wasn’t… one big… hard thing.” She pointed at the outermost line. “This is what you’ve got now. A… shell. If the storm pushes, it has to hold. Or it breaks.” Her fingers traced the concentric, more flexible rings she’d doodled. “But if it was more like… I don’t know. A… net. Or a jellyfish bell. Layers. You could… let some of the storm through. Bleed it off. Instead of… taking it all here.”

The kettle gave an alarmed “wheeeeep” at the word bleed.

“Yes, yes, yes,” Finn said, ignoring it. “A permeable barrier. A… semi-permeable membrane.” His eyes lit the way they did whenever biology snuck into engineering. “Filters instead of walls. Letting in what we need, pushing out what we don’t.”

He tapped the paper. “But how would it know the difference between a… safe current and a dangerous one? Between a friendly dolphin and a trawler?”

Mara’s pencil hovered. “That’s… the problem,” she admitted. “We’d need… rules. Patterns. Something that tells the Veil ‘this kind of movement is okay, this isn’t.’”

“Language,” Liora said.

They both looked at her.

She sat up, tail curling under her, that inward-listening expression on her face again. The one she’d worn when they’d first stepped into her Library of Whispers. As if half her attention was on something no one else could hear.

“The Whale… spoke about songs,” she said. “The way currents know where to run because they’ve always run there. Because something sang them that way. His songs. Madam’s. Ours, once.” Her tail flicked against the floor, faster now, matching some remembered pulse. “The Veil’s just… another song. Right now it’s… shouting one word: NO. Over and over. At everything.”

Mara winced. She could almost hear it: a constant, droning note that didn’t care what it was drowning out.

“What if we taught it… more words?” Liora mused. “Different rhythms. Codes. Ways to say… ‘let this through, hide that; bend here, hold there.’”

“Stories,” Mara said, almost at the same time.

Finn raised his eyebrows. “Stories?”

Mara flapped her pencil, trying to catch the idea as it tried to flee.

“On land,” she said, “when we… don’t know what to do, or how to be… with something big, we… tell stories about it. We have… weather-lore. Sailors’ sayings. ‘Red sky at night, shepherd’s delight.’ ‘Mackerel scales and mare’s tails make lofty ships carry low sails.’”

The translation shell tangled itself briefly, struggling with the idioms, then draped a version of them over Liora and Finn’s understanding as: red-sky calm-warning, fish-cloud caution, lower your fins when the high-wind shapes appear.

“We… encode patterns,” Mara said. “In words. In… songs. So you don’t have to know… the meteorology. You just… feel the proverb and act. What if the Veil’s… instructions… weren’t just numbers in your engine, Professor? What if they were… patterns. Rhythms. Stories. Like… teaching it cultural etiquette instead of just… physics.”

The kettle gave a low, uncertain whistle, as if torn between warning and curiosity.

Finn’s beard fluffed. “You want to… program the shield-engine with… folk wisdom.”

“Is that bad?” Mara asked.

“It’s… gloriously bad,” he said. “Deeply suspect. Elegantly foolish.”

Liora’s eyes glittered. “It makes sense,” she said. “Our engine’s already… humming. Madam’s already… singing. The Whale’s… complaining in chords. If we keep treating the Veil like a… machine in a box, we’ll keep… scraping against them. But if we treat it like… another voice in the choir…”

“We weave its hum out of… songs,” Finn finished, half to himself. “Layered patterns. Structural as well as narrative. The Engine would still shape currents, but instead of a blunt ‘push everything away from the bubble,’ it would… listen. It would… harmonise. Or try.”

His hands were already moving, grabbing a scrap of algae-paper of his own, scratching circles and notes onto it: a schematised Veil, the Engine, the Whale, Madam Ocean herself sketched as a spiralling, central arrow.

“We already know certain songs affect local currents,” Liora said. “The Tide-Callers do it. They sing into the stones. The old work crews used to chant while laying coral. Professor, you told my class once that the first Engine prototype only started working when someone”—she coughed delicately; everyone in the room knew it had been Finn himself—“swore at it in three different dialects and a minor key.”

“I maintain that was coincidental,” Finn said. “But yes. Sound matters. Not just pressure. Not just flow. Meaning, perhaps, in a way we don’t quite… quantify yet.”

Mara looked back at her mess of curls and loops.

“We could… start small,” she said. “Not rewriting the whole Veil at once. Just… some parts.” She circled one section of her doodled ‘curtain.’ “Here. The layers… nearest Pelagoria. Instead of just saying ‘no,’ they could say… ‘slow.’” She drew tight little loops. “They… tangle fast things. Blunt impacts. But…” She left gaps. “They don’t… trap. They just… delay. Spread.”

“You’re describing a… boundary layer,” Finn murmured. “Viscous drag. Turbulence damping. That we can do. That’s… already in the Engine. But you want it to… change based on… what’s hitting it.”

“Like a story reacting to who’s listening,” Mara said. “You don’t tell a three-year-old the same ghost story you tell a teenager. If you’re… kind.”

Liora grinned. “So we teach the Veil to… tell different stories to different things. If it hears the pattern of a trawler’s propellers, it sings one way. If it hears… the Lantern Whale’s song, or… friendly fish, it sings another.”

Finn’s eyes lit properly now, the way they did when nine impossible concepts finally linked into something mathematically juicy.

“A pattern-recognition layer,” he said. “An acoustic signature filter. We’d need… listening shells embedded along the outer membrane. To hear. To classify. To feed that into the Engine. And the Engine would need a… new library of responses.”

He looked around his chaotic workshop as if expecting a library to materialise out of guilt.

“You have a library,” Mara said promptly. “Liora’s. Full of… forbidden stories and old songs.”

Liora sat up straighter. “We could… use the coded shells,” she said. “The ones with recorded chants. Whale-songs. Current-calls. The ones you said we weren’t meant to crack open without a grown-up.”

She folded her arms. “You count as a grown-up. Sort of.”

Finn spluttered. “Those are… untested. You can’t just… pour random cultural artefacts into a sensitive machine.”

“You already did,” Liora pointed out. “You poured your fear of nets and your mother’s anger and everyone’s caution into it. You just didn’t admit that’s what you were doing.”

The kettle let out a long, mournful keen. Finn swatted its spout until it subsided.

“All right,” he said. “Point taken. The Engine is already full of stories. They’re just… all one kind. ‘Hide.’ ‘Repel.’ ‘No.’” His mouth twisted. “We need… more complicated ones. ‘Not here.’ ‘Not that.’ ‘Slow down.’ ‘Come in, but gently.’”

Mara’s heart thumped, excitement battling terror.

“We could… encode them,” she said. “Like… knots. Sailor’s knots.” She’d seen a book in Thimble’s museum once, full of diagrams: bowline, clove hitch, reef knot. Each with its little song to remember it by. “On land, we used to… memorise things with… rhymes. Liora can… translate into current patterns. You can… turn those into… gear timings. Water pulses.”

“Oh, can I?” Finn said. But he didn’t sound affronted; he sounded intrigued. “We’d need… stable anchors. The Engine’s main gears. They hum at fixed frequencies.” He tapped the air as if plucking invisible strings. “We can overlay other rhythms on that. Liora, the Whale’s last song—can you remember its… contour?”

Liora closed her eyes. Her tailtip flicked, then began tapping again.

This time the rhythm was more complex: long pulses, short pulses, a rising pattern, a long held beat. Her fingers joined, drumming on the floor in a syncopated counterpoint.

The workshop seemed to lean in.

Mara felt the shape of it in her chest. Not as clearly as the Whale’s song in the trench, but a ghost of it. Her heart wanted to follow it, slotting its own lub-dub into the spaces between.

Finn’s hands twitched, mapping the rhythm unconsciously. “Good,” he murmured. “Yes. That… there. If we feed that through the Engine’s primary valves instead of the current flatline… the harmonics would shift. The hum would… match more of Madam’s background song. The Veil might… stop fighting quite so hard at the edges.”

“And if we weave in…” Liora groped for a word. “…home-songs.”

Mara blinked. “Home-songs?”

“The chants we use when we lay coral,” Liora said. “The ones the builders hum when setting stone. The lullabies mothers sing to make babies sleep through storm surges. The patterns our hearts already know.”

She thumped her tail again. For a few beats, the rhythm changed: gentler, rocking. Mara thought of someone swaying in shallow water with a child in their arms.

“We could… synchronise,” Finn said slowly. “Use the Heartstones.”

“The what?” Mara said.

Finn pointed at the floor. “Every major structure in Pelagoria has a Heartstone,” he said. “A chunk of thick, resonant coral fused into its foundations. It carries vibrations. It lets us know if something is… wrong. Cracks. Strains. Extra weight.” He tapped his own chest. “We can… monitor them. In theory.”

“In theory?” the kettle asked shrilly.

“In theory and practice which is ongoing,” Finn corrected. “What if, instead of just listening for damage, we sent rhythm down?” His eyes gleamed. “We could… cascade. Start the new hum in the Engine. Feed it into a few key Heartstones. Let the city’s own… footsteps reinforce it.” His gaze slid to Liora. “And their… heartbeats.”

“You want the whole city to… sing the shield into a new shape,” Liora said, half-marvelling, half-horrified. “At once.”

“Yes,” Finn said. “Briefly. Carefully. With supervision.”

“That’s… impossible,” Bubbles quavered.

“Good,” Finn said. “Impossible things are where all the interesting mistakes live.”

Mara’s pencil moved almost of its own accord, adding to the loops on the page: tiny, repeated motifs. She found herself humming under her breath, a snatch of a tune her mother used to sing washing dishes when storms rattled their windows.

She didn’t even realise she’d done it aloud until Liora said, “What’s that?”

Mara stopped. “Oh. Just… a song. My mum’s.”

“Words?” Liora asked.

Mara flushed. “It’s silly,” she said. “It’s about… rain. And… cups of tea. And… staying inside until the worst blows over.”

“The Veil likes that,” Finn observed. “Too much.”

“It’s not just that,” Mara protested. “It’s… about… how storms pass. How… you’re still there after. How the same roof that keeps the rain out… also keeps you in.” She struggled. “We could… use that. Inverse it. Teach the Veil… that letting some things through… is part of… not breaking.”

Liora’s eyes softened. “Sing it,” she said.

Mara grimaced. “Absolutely not.”

“Just a bit,” Liora cajoled. “For… pattern. I won’t tell anyone your voice cracked.”

“My voice does not crack,” Mara said, stung. “It… squeaks. Occasionally. In dignified protest.”

Finn, already at a nearby chalkboard, scribbling new equations that looked suspiciously like music notation, said absently, “We don’t need pitch. We need rhythm. Words. The structure. We can… abstract.”

Mara stared at the algae-paper, mortified. Then she remembered the Whale’s lanterns, the lines of strain along his flank. The thin crack of light that had opened near the top of Finn’s graph.

“Fine,” she said.

She sang.

Her voice came out small in the big, cluttered workshop, but steady. The words were simple, the melody lilting: an old tune, passed down through kitchens and damp coat stands, meant to make scrubbing plates feel less like labour and more like a spell.

Tip-tap on the window, rain on the glass,  
Boil up the kettle, let the dark clouds pass.  
Roof over, floor under, walls standing fast,  
Storm in the doorway, you can’t come in last.

She faltered, cheeks heating. “It gets… sillier after that,” she muttered.

But Liora was already tapping the rhythm with her tail, brow furrowed. “It’s… like our storm-lullabies,” she said. “Different notes, same… shape.” She tapped out a counter-rhythm, fitting it against Mara’s beats. “If we… lay this under the Whale’s song… here…” She marked a point on Finn’s chalkboard with one finger. “Where his long note dips. The Veil might… rock instead of… brace.”

Finn added another line of marks. “Rocking is good,” he said. “Structures that rock don’t crack as easily as ones that insist on being still. Madam likes rocking. The Engine can… be taught.”

The kettle gave a low, doubtful hiss. Finn flipped its little lid up and down as if shushing a toddler.

“So,” Mara said slowly, looking from board to paper to Engine. “We… retune the Engine to hum a pattern that… includes the Whale’s deep song, our… home-songs, my… storm-song. We feed that through the Heartstones. The city… responds. For a while. Enough to… change the Veil’s… shape.”

“And we build into that hum,” Liora added, “a set of… listening rules. The Engine’s… pattern-recognition. Stories about… what’s safe and what isn’t. We use your scary graphs,” she nodded at Finn, “to tell it when the Lantern Whale is… near, so it opens. When trawler-sounds appear, so it… thickens. When storms slam down, so it… yields at the top and… holds at the bottom.”

“Yielding at the top,” Finn repeated, scribbling furiously. “Yes. Let some energy pass through. Dissipate. We can… bleed it sideways, around the city. Make the Veil… bulge instead of… crack.”

The kettle squealed at bleed again. He silenced it with a firm twist of a valve.

“There are… problems,” Bubbles piped up, voice tinny from a nearby speaker shell. “Major flaws. Catastrophic holes. You are all pretending not to see them.”

“Go on, then,” Liora said. “Be the sensible one.”

Bubbles huffed. “Fine. First: Professor Finn’s Engine is… twitchy. You tune it wrong, it might… hum off-key, confuse Madam, and tear the Veil anyway. Second: you’re talking about synchronising an entire city’s… heartbeats. People might not… cooperate. Or they might, and it might still… misfire. Third: you intend to pour unknown sonic patterns from untested cultural archives into a device currently preventing several million tons of water from sitting on your heads. Have I missed anything?”

“Only that we’ll be doing all this… while the Surface continues to throw storms at us and trawlers at the Whale,” Finn said. “Small footnote.”

“Exactly,” Bubbles said. “This is not… sensible.”

“Good,” Liora said again. “Sensible is what got us here.”

Mara stared back down at her paper. Her swirls and loops looked less ridiculous now. Messy, yes. But… alive.

“It is… risky,” Finn conceded. “Yes. We are proposing to replace a relatively stable, if harmful, wrong thing with a possibly unstable, possibly less harmful, possibly differently wrong thing. From an engineering standpoint, this is… a nightmare.”

He straightened, chalk clenched in one hand, algae-sheet in the other. His beard haloed out around his face like a cloud of startled thoughts.

“From a moral standpoint,” he said, “we are already doing harm by—” He waved vaguely upward. “—squeezing the sea. Doing nothing is a decision too. A very safe-feeling one with very unsafe consequences. More power, more hum, more of the same is… worse. Leaving is… worse in a different flavour. So.” He slapped his algae-sheet down on the bench. “We arrive at… this.”

He gestured to Mara’s drawing, to Liora’s remembered rhythms, to the chalkboard spiderweb of equations and arrows.

“Technically unsound,” he said, with a kind of reluctant admiration. “Morally ambiguous. In violation of at least four Council edicts and probably two natural laws.”

He smiled then, a crooked, bright, reckless thing.

“And therefore,” he concluded, “in my professional opinion, our best option.”

The kettle, perhaps sensing the shift from hypothetical danger to chosen risk, let out one last feeble whistle and then, astonishingly, settled.

Mara felt the idea—the Plan That Wasn’t Sensible—solidify in her chest.

It was absurd. Of course it was. They were going to fix a failing ocean shield by teaching it to sing better. They were going to ask a whole city to trust them long enough to let their heartbeats be used as tuning-forks. They were going to poke at Madam Ocean’s patience with a bunch of stories and hope she appreciated the effort.

But the alternative was to sit there while the Veil tightened and the Whale winced and the storms pounded and the cracks widened.

“I’ll… have to go home,” Mara said, the thought falling into place with a weighty inevitability. “Soon. To… watch. To see if the surface… changes. If the storms feel… different. If the tidepool Door… behaves.”

“And to bring us… new stories,” Liora added. “Sky-patterns. Those… proverbs. ‘Mackerel scales.’ ‘Mare’s tails.’ The way the wind smells before a storm. We don’t… have that up here.Our Veil needs that… perspective.”

“And I will need,” Finn said, “to make the Engine… receptive. To build in the listening shells. To talk Queen Nerissa into letting me take apart my own greatest achievement in front of the entire Council. Preferably without being exiled to the outer algae farms.”

“And I,” Liora said, “will need to talk the city into… helping. Library-smugglers. Tide-callers. Mothers with lullabies. Grumpy elders who know old work songs. We’ll need them all. Or the Heartstones will just hum with… nonsense.”

Mara’s pencil drew one more line: a simple, connecting one between two loops. It was a clumsy little stroke. But it bridged the gap.

Her own fear sat beside her curiosity, not gone, but… reorganised.

“I’ll do it,” she said. “I’ll… go back. And come down again. And tell you what I see. And tell… them… about you. The Whale. Madam. The Veil. Even if they… laugh.”

“They will,” Liora said. “Some of them.”

“Some of them won’t,” Mara said. “Some… will hear. You said so yourself. They don’t all hum the same.”

“Nice phrase,” Finn murmured. “Must steal that for a paper.”

The workshop seemed, in some small, indefinable way, to approve.

The Engine’s hum smoothed just a fraction, as if encouraged by the promise of new songs. The bubbles drifting along the ceiling slowed their nervous popping. The kettle stopped whistling entirely and released, at last, a small, apologetic puff of steam that smelled faintly of over-brewed tea.

Rain still pummelled the world above. The Whale still glided along the edges of trenches, feeling the strain in his lanterns. The Veil still shuddered under storm-strikes.

But here, in a cluttered room full of half-built things and too many ideas, three unlikely conspirators had chosen a path that was neither more wall nor simple flight.

They would widen.

They would weave.

They would ask a humming machine and a stubborn sea to learn a new kind of story—one made of lullabies and weather-sayings and whale-chants and the shaky, unapproved conviction of a child from above and a child from below that safety built only on fear could never really hold.

“Right,” Finn said briskly, as if they had just agreed on something minor, like dinner. “First step: test. Small-scale. We find one Heartstone, one little corner of the Veil. We try humming at it differently. See if it… ripples. No citywide catastrophes until at least the second trial. Agreed?”

“Agreed,” Liora said.

Mara swallowed. “Agreed.”

Bubbles sighed dramatically. “If I explode, I’m haunting all of you,” it said.

“You’d have to queue,” Finn told it. “Now. Who knows a good storm-lullaby in nine-eight time?”

Liora’s tail began to tap again. Mara found herself humming. Finn reached for his tools.

Outside, the sea rolled and rumbled. Inside, three hand-hewn hearts and one semi-sentient bubble of engineering started, in their own small, not-at-all-sensible way, to retune the hum of a city.
