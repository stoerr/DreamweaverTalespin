# Professor Finn’s Impossible Machine

Where Mara and Liora visit Professor Barnaby Finn, whose peculiar invention has started to malfunction and may be connected to the weakening of Pelagoria’s shield.

Pelagoria, Mara decided, was a city that liked to pretend it was calm.

From high up in the palace hall, looking out through the great curve of glass and coral, it had all looked serene: domes glowing softly, currents looping in graceful patterns, tiny fish slipping through bands of structured water as if they’d rehearsed their routes.

From down here, slipping between those patterns with Liora at her side, it felt different. Busier. Nervier. Like a pond that looked still until you stepped in and realised the whole thing was full of things darting just out of sight.

They had left the palace the official way this time—or at least, Mara assumed it was official, given the number of bows Liora got and the number of disapproving looks Mara attracted from people with very clean collars.

“Walk like you’re supposed to be there,” Liora had muttered as they passed through the coral arch into the wider city. “It confuses adults.”

“What if I’m not supposed to be here?” Mara whispered back.

“Then you should walk even more like you are,” Liora said. “Trust me. Authority hates confusion.”

Now they were well clear of the High Hall, moving through one of the upper districts that clung to the inside of the Pelagorian bubble like barnacles to a ship’s hull.

Here, the walkways were narrower, branching and looping without the stately curves of the palace bridges. The domes were smaller, clustered more tightly, their walls bearing the faint scuff-marks and algae streaks of everyday life. Lines of tiny, bobbing lantern-creatures hung across alleys. The distant hum of the palace machinery gave way to more local sounds: the hiss of water through pipes, the clatter of shell against shell from a workshop, the faint, echoing call of someone selling something that smelled strongly of brine and something only almost like garlic.

“We’re not going to be missed?” Mara asked, dodging a low-hanging strand of what might have been laundry and might have been experimental seaweed.

“Oh, we absolutely are going to be missed,” Liora said. “I left a note.”

“A note,” Mara repeated.

“Yes,” Liora said. “It says: ‘Gone to study. Do not worry. Not in danger (yet). Love, L.’ Very accurate.”

Mara considered this. “Will they believe you?”

“They will believe I went to study,” Liora said. “Which is true.”

A jellyfish the size of a carriage drifted past on the other side of the dome wall, its long, trailing tentacles streaming behind like the train of a very nervous bride’s gown. Its bell pulsed with a slow, steady rhythm. As it went by, the air in the district seemed to pause, watching.

“Why are they looking at us?” Mara murmured, aware of eyes tracking her from doorways and windows as they walked.

“Because you’re new,” Liora said. “And dry. And your hair is doing that surface thing.”

“What surface thing?”

“The…” Liora waved her fingers vaguely around Mara’s head. “The… halo. Like you stuck your fingers in a static eel.”

Mara clapped a self-conscious hand over her curls. Their brief, hurried journey through the palace and into the city had not improved them. Strands escaped her ponytail in all directions, drifting faintly in the thin air, refusing to decide which way was down.

“It’s not that bad,” she muttered.

“It’s loud,” Liora said, with the bluntness of someone who had grown up around people with very smooth hydrodynamics. “But it’s useful. If we get separated, I can just look for the cloud.”

Mara made a face. It was a good face. Unfortunately, a group of younger children, scaling a nearby railing like a school of monkeys chasing a particularly interesting current, saw it and burst into giggles.

“Surface,” one of them whispered, wide-eyed. “Look, look, she’s got no fins.”

“Her poor ankles,” another breathed. “So exposed.”

“Don’t stare,” an older woman hissed, dragging them back. “You’ll make the shield flicker.”

“That’s not how the shield works,” Liora said under her breath, with the long-suffering patience of someone who had explained this before and not been believed. “If staring broke it, the whole city would be in fragments.”

They turned down a narrower lane.

Here, the domes gave way to more irregular structures: little stacks of carved stone and fused shell, their edges softened by creeping coral. Odd lights blinked from round windows—colours that had no business being produced by anything natural. The hum in the air changed, became higher, chattier, broken into pockets. Mara could feel it in her teeth.

“Is this… a workshop area?” she asked.

“It’s the Tinker Quarter,” Liora said, with a peculiar mixture of fondness and exasperation. “Artificers. Mechanics. People who think ‘stable’ is an insult.”

A small bubble, no bigger than her fist, drifted past Mara’s face. It contained, impossibly, a tiny, slowly rotating cube of light. As she watched, the cube unfolded into a flower shape, then folded back into a cube again. The bubble bumped gently against her nose, decided she was uninteresting, and moved on.

“Professor Finn lives three bends down,” Liora said. “And if we’re very unlucky, he’s in one of his Focus Moods, in which case we may have to be loud. Or throw something.”

“I thought you liked Professor Finn,” Mara said.

“I do,” Liora said. “He taught me that you can boil water with pressure and that some currents can be tricked into looping on themselves if you compliment them enough. He also once accidentally flooded the Council Hall with coloured steam because he ‘slightly misjudged’ a thermal exchange.” She sighed, but there was admiration in it. “Come on.”

They rounded another curve, and there it was.

Professor Barnaby Finn’s workshop looked as if a thoughtful shipwreck had sprouted.

It bulged out from the side of a larger structure, its walls a patchwork of different materials: slabs of stone fused with patches of clear bubble-glass, ribs of coral stitched together with bands of metal that had no name in Mara’s vocabulary. Pipes of various widths emerged from its sides and dove back in, some gurgling, some quiet, some ticking faintly.

Above the wide, round doorway, a sign had been carved into a shell and bolted on at a slightly crooked angle. The flowing Pelagorian script shimmered faintly. The translation shell gave Mara: BARNABY FINN, CURRENTS ENGINEER, PROBABLE GENIUS. (APPOINTMENTS UNADVISED.)

“‘Probable,’” Mara said. “That’s… reassuring.”

“It used to say ‘Possible,’” Liora said. “He upgraded it after the shield went in.”

Two small bubbles hovered at either side of the door, glowing a gentle green. As Mara watched, one of them flickered, spitting out a crackle of blue light before righting itself.

“That’s not supposed to happen,” Liora muttered. “Good. We’re in the right place.”

She didn’t knock. She simply ducked her head and walked in. Mara, after a brief, resigned moment in which she accepted that her life had moved permanently into the column marked Unscheduled, followed.

The inside of the workshop was larger than it had any right to be, which at this point Mara suspected was just Pelagoria’s architectural style.

The ceiling was high and domed, its curve lined with hanging nets. In the nets, objects dangled: glass floats, gears, the occasional bewildered-looking shell. Bubbles drifted among them in slow, lazy currents, some tiny, some as big as Mara’s head. Each bubble held something: a small, ticking mechanism; a folded bit of algae-paper; a miniature lightning storm.

Workbenches grew from the floor in organic, uneven clusters, their surfaces an explosion of tools and half-finished contraptions. Coils of tubing snaked across the room, some transparent and full of water racing in different directions, others pulsing with light. Gears turned lazily in woven harnesses of kelp and thread, their teeth made of shell and pearl, their centres studded with little coral growths.

The air smelled of salt and hot metal and something else—sharp and tangy, like the moment you peel back the lid on a bottle of fizzy drink.

At the far end of the room, hunched over a contraption that looked, to Mara’s eyes, like a teapot that had been unfortunate enough to wander into a nest of octopi, was a man.

He wore a patched diving-suit of a sort Mara had never seen before. It looked like someone had decided a normal suit was insufficient and added extra bits whenever they thought of them. Plates of shell reinforced the chest and shoulders. Flexible bands of some rubbery material wrapped elbows and knees. Tubes sprouted from the back and arced overhead like a skeletal, translucent rucksack, ending in small, fluttering fans that whirred feebly.

His beard was magnificent.

It floated around his chin in a cloud of white and grey, as if it had long ago given up trying to obey gravity and instead decided to stage its own weather system. Bits of something glinted in it: wire, perhaps, or small tools that had gotten trapped and been forgotten.

Mara had time to notice all of this because neither the Professor nor the beard were currently paying them the slightest attention.

“Now, now, just a little nudge,” he muttered to himself, bending close to the octo-teapot device. His fingers, long and stained with oil, adjusted a small coral dial. “If we can just get you to hum at the right frequency, my dear, you might finally understand Thursdays…”

The device—an elegant abomination of pearlescent metal and articulated limbs—made a noise like a kettle considering a scream, then released a small jet of glittering steam straight into his face.

Professor Finn reeled back, coughing, his beard briefly illuminated from within like a storm-cloud backlit by lightning.

“Ha!” he wheezed, blinking tears out of his eyes. “Progress!”

“Professor?” Liora said, loudly enough to compete with the general background hum.

He didn’t turn around. He waved a hand vaguely in their direction, nearly smacking a passing bubble and sending it wobbling uncertainly.

“Not now, not now, very busy, the tide is at a crucial angle and the cogs are sulking,” he said. “Leave any questions in the third drawer on the left, marked ‘Later.’ Or was it ‘Possibly Never’? I always forget.”

“Barnaby,” Liora said, with the patient force of someone who was used to navigating this particular storm. “You have a surfacer in your workshop.”

“Mm-hm,” Professor Finn said. “Very interesting, my dear, very interesting. I’ll have a look at it once I’ve—”

He froze.

Slowly, the man straightened. His beard puffed out in unconscious alarm, making his head briefly resemble a startled dandelion.

He turned.

His eyes were blue—not the sharp, sailor’s blue of Captain Thimble’s, but a milder, washier shade, like ink someone had tried to water down and then used anyway. They widened as they landed on Mara, taking in, in rapid succession: her hair, her clothes, the sand still clinging in stubborn specks to her shoelaces.

He dropped the tool he was holding.

It bounced off the workbench, ricocheted off a small gear assembly, and vanished into a cluster of tubing with a faint, metallic “ploink.”

“A surface child,” he breathed. “In… here.”

“Yes,” Mara said, because there seemed to be no point in argument. “Sorry about the… mess?”

“The mess is mine,” he said absently, then, with a sudden, jarringly bright grin: “Marvelous! Welcome! Don’t touch anything that looks like it might object, and if you feel a sudden urge to sneeze backwards, duck.”

Mara blinked. “I—what?”

“Barnaby,” Liora said, stepping between them before Mara’s brain could dissolve. “Focus.”

“Oh, it’s you,” Professor Finn said, noticing her belatedly. “Hello, Princess. How are your currents? Still rebellious?”

“Always,” Liora said. “And it’s Liora, not ‘Princess.’ And this is Mara. She fell through the Queen’s Door this morning. Nearly drowned in narrative complications.”

“Did she?” Professor Finn said faintly. “Excellent. Terrible. Terribly excellent.” He scrubbed at his face, leaving a smear of something metallic across his cheek. “Has Her Majesty…?”

“She’s asking questions,” Liora said. “And not telling me the answers. So I’m asking my own. Starting here.” She waved a hand at the cluttered workshop. “With you. And your… things.”

Professor Finn beamed. “My things! Yes. Most of them work. Some of them work as something else. A few of them refuse to work unless you don’t want them to. Which is a philosophical achievement, really.”

“We think something is wrong with the shield,” Liora said, cutting through his enthusiasm with the precision of a scalpel. “The Veil. The city’s protections. The Lantern Whale’s songs are off, the currents are twitchy, and a Door opened for someone it shouldn’t have. You helped build the shield. If it’s breaking, I thought you might notice before anyone else stops pretending it’s fine.”

Professor Finn opened his mouth. Closed it again. His gaze flicked to Mara, to Liora, back to Mara.

“Did you,” he said slowly, “by any chance, sign a waiver before coming here?”

“A what?” Mara said.

“A waiver,” he repeated. “A formal acknowledgment that whatever happens in my general vicinity is both temporally and narratively unstable and that you accept the risk of mild confusion, occasional déjà vu, and the very remote possibility of being in two places at once for up to three seconds.”

“No,” Mara said. “Should I… have?”

“She’s ten,” Liora said. “She shouldn’t have to sign anything.”

“Everyone should sign waivers,” Professor Finn said seriously. “Life is very badly signposted. Still. We’ll proceed without, but if you find yourself remembering things that haven’t happened yet, do let me know. It would be terribly useful.”

He wiped his hands on his patched suit, leaving twin streaks of oil, and then clapped them together briskly.

“The shield,” he said. “Ah. The Veil. The Great Hush. Our shared delusion of safety. Yes. You think something’s wrong with it.”

“We know something is wrong with it,” Liora said. “The Door opened.”

“Yes, but doors open,” Professor Finn said. “That’s what they do. It’s in the job description.”

“They don’t open on their own,” Liora said. “Not that Door. Not after… what happened.”

Professor Finn’s face shuttered, briefly, at that, his eyes going distant.

“No,” he agreed quietly. “Not after that.”

He shook himself, beard fluffing, and pointed at Mara.

“And you, young…?”

“Mara,” she supplied. “Mara Bright.”

“Miss Bright,” he said. “You fell through the Door. Unprotected.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “It wasn’t really my idea.”

“They rarely are,” Professor Finn said. “Doors can be very pushy. How did you breathe?”

Mara hesitated. “I… just did,” she said. “The water felt… different. Not like normal water. Softer. Or… friendlier. It didn’t go up my nose. Much. I could hold my breath for longer than usual. And when I…”

She gestured at the floor, miming a vague plummeting motion.

“…arrived, there was air,” she finished lamely. “In the bubble. And it wasn’t… drowning air. It was okay.”

Professor Finn stared at her as if she’d just casually recited the formula for untangling currents.

“Remarkable,” he murmured. “I always suspected there might be self-adjusting properties in the threshold matrix…”

“Barnaby,” Liora said again, more sharply.

“Right, right.” He rubbed his hands together, brisk now. “The shield. The Veil. The thing what keeps us out of sight and out of mind. You are quite right, Your… Liora. I helped Her Majesty construct it. Or rather, I helped build the machine that keeps it humming. Madam Ocean did the rest.”

“Madam…?” Mara said.

“The sea,” Liora and Professor Finn said together.

“Oh,” Mara said. “Her. Yes. I’ve heard about her.”

“She has opinions,” Finn said gravely. “The shield is a negotiation. We ask the sea to bend around us in certain ways. She agrees, mostly, as long as we don’t break the old rules. No nets, no hooks, no noisy propellers. No calling attention. In return, she lets the Veil sit in her currents and redirect prying eyes.”

“And the engine?” Liora said.

“Ah.” Professor Finn’s face lit. “The engine is my bit.”

He gestured expansively at a large, partially obscured shape occupying the back corner of the workshop. Until he moved, Mara had thought it was simply another haphazard mound of equipment. Now, as he cleared a path with surprising agility, she saw it for what it was.

For a certain, slightly alarming value of “what it was.”

The Veil engine looked like someone had taken the idea of a clock, a watermill, and a glassblower’s studio, thrown them into a whirlpool, and then built whatever had clumped together at the end.

A broad, circular base of fused sand anchored it. From that base, several thick, curved columns of translucent material rose, not straight but spiraling gently, like the bones of some enormous shell. Between these columns stretched membranes of water held in impossible shapes: sheets that hung without falling, twisted tubes that looped in and out of each other, all shot through with threads of light that pulsed in slow, intricate patterns.

Around and through these water-forms moved gears of various sizes, their teeth made of shell, their centres crystallised into strange, shimmering densities. Some turned steadily, others ticked forward a tooth at a time and then paused, as if thinking. Tiny, bell-like chimes were attached at intervals; when a gear completed a circuit, it nudged one, producing a soft, clear ring.

Above it all, a sort of crown of branches arced, made of braided coral and thin rods of metal that glowed faintly. They dipped into the hanging water-planes here and there, pulling little streams out and redirecting them.

It all hummed—a low, complex tone that made Mara’s ribs buzz. It was not a machine for making anything as mundane as power or heat. It seemed to be reshaping something more fundamental.

“Behold,” Professor Finn said, with the pride of someone introducing a slightly unruly pet. “The Current Harmoniser. The Veil Engine. The Great Big Thing That Goes Hummmm.”

Mara stepped closer. The hairs on her arms stood up. The air around the engine felt thicker, like it had been folded a few extra times.

“How does it… work?” she asked, mesmerised by the slow pulse of light through the hanging water. Tiny motes—sand? air?—sparkled within it.

“In theory?” Professor Finn said. “Beautifully.”

“In practice?” Liora asked.

“Temperamentally,” he admitted. “It reshapes currents at the smallest level—just enough to create a sort of… edgeless edge around us. It persuades water molecules to remember different paths. It whispers to turbulence and says, ‘Not here, thank you, go bother a cliff instead.’ It is very polite. Mostly.”

Mara watched one of the hanging sheets of water. As she stared, its surface shivered, then briefly showed a reflection that was not of the workshop: a grey, rain-dark sky; a row of breaking waves; the suggestion of a distant shoreline.

Then it smoothed again, the image dissolving.

“That looked like the surface,” she blurted.

“Yes, yes, it does that,” Professor Finn said. “Leakage. Echoes. Perfectly harmless. Usually. Though it does confuse the cleaning staff.”

“Leakage?” Liora repeated, incredulous. “You built a machine to hold back the entire Outside and you’re okay with it… leaking?”

“Well, you can’t have a barrier without a bit of bleed,” Finn said. “Boundaries are messy things. But lately…” He frowned, rubbing the bridge of his nose. “Lately, it has been… more enthusiastic.”

He shuffled over to a nearby bench and rummaged among stacks of algae-paper and little discs. After a moment, he produced a flat panel studded with small, iridescent shells.

He propped it on a slanting stand and tapped one shell. The surface of the panel rippled, then blossomed with a series of lines.

At first, Mara thought they were random squiggles. Then her brain recognised the shape of a graph: time along the bottom, something else up the side. Several lines undulated across it, each in a different faint colour—blue, green, pale gold. They rose and fell with a certain rough regularity.

“This is the Veil’s output,” Finn said, warming to his subject. “Each line represents a different aspect of the shield. Density. Phase-hum. Emotional tenor of nearby currents. The usual.”

Liora gave him a look that said very clearly that nothing in that sentence was usual.

“For years,” Finn went on, tapping one section of the graph where the lines jogged along with mild, predictable squiggles, “it has been wonderfully boring. A few little spikes when there’s a storm. A dip or two when Madam Ocean has a sulk. Nothing alarming. But in the last few months…”

He pointed to a section nearer the right-hand edge.

The lines there were not calm. They spiked up and down, some in tall, narrow leaps, others in wide, uneven waves. There were places where two lines crossed that had not intersected before. A faint dotted band at the top—some threshold—quivered, occasionally brushed by a jump from below.

Liora sucked in a breath. “That’s… bad,” she said. “Isn’t it?”

“Oh, well, ‘bad’ is such a strong word,” Professor Finn hedged. “I prefer ‘energetic.’ Or ‘expressive.’ Or ‘statistically interesting.’”

“When did it start?” Liora demanded.

Finn scratched at his beard, dislodging a small spring that pinged onto the floor and rolled away in a sulky spiral.

“Approximately… nine Whale-cycles ago,” he said. “Possibly ten. My notes are a bit… damp.” He flapped a hand at a nearby pile of algae-sheets covered in tiny, spidery writing. Some were indeed smudged, as if someone had taken them for a swim.

“Nine cycles,” Liora said. “That’s when the Lantern Whale’s songs started coming late.”

“Is it?” Finn said vaguely. “Oh, how synchronised of him.”

“And about when Madam Ocean began having… moods,” Liora added. “More storms. Unseasonal ones. And Captain Th—” She caught herself. “And some surface people started noticing.”

Mara thought of Captain Thimble Kelp shaking his head at the unseasonable storm. Of the way the museum floor had seemed to breathe under her hand.

“You don’t think that’s… connected?” she asked.

Finn opened his mouth to say something reassuring and meaningless. Liora, watching him with narrowed eyes, cut in.

“Show her the last door-event,” she said.

Finn winced. “Is that necessary?”

“Yes,” Liora said. “Or we can let her drown slowly in lack of context.”

Finn sighed, the sound rattling his tools on their hooks. He tapped another shell on the panel.

A new line bloomed across the graph, this one in a sharper, angrier red. It was flat for a time, hugging the bottom. Then, abruptly, it shot up, slashed jaggedly across the others, and held there for a worrying stretch before dropping back.

“That,” Finn said, “is your tidepool.”

Mara stared. “That’s… when I fell?”

“That’s when the Door opened,” Finn said. “Properly. Without a key. Without my… coaxing. It’s supposed to be a controlled aperture. The engine knows its song. The Queen knows hers. This—” He tapped the spike. “—is not controlled.”

“And you still called it ‘leakage’?” Liora demanded.

“I was hoping,” Finn said weakly, “if I used a nice, small word, the problem would feel obliged to be nice and small.”

Mara leaned closer. Her eyes traced the lines, the peaks, the dips. She frowned.

“Have you noticed,” she said slowly, “that the spikes aren’t random?”

“Hmm?” Finn said.

“They’re… bunched,” Mara said. “Look.” She pointed. “Here. And here. And that one.” Her finger hovered over several clusters where the red line—door-activity, she assumed—leapt, each cluster roughly the same distance apart.

“And between them,” she went on, “these…” She touched the blue and green lines, which, between the clusters, rippled in a subtle, almost wave-like pattern. “…do this. Like they’re… being pulled. Or… pushed from underneath.”

Professor Finn squinted, leaning so close his nose nearly brushed the panel. Several of the shells flickered, indignant.

“Intervals,” he murmured. “Regular-ish intervals. I did wonder about that. I thought perhaps my measuring shells were having a little joke. They do that sometimes. They have a poor sense of humour.”

Liora’s hand darted in from the other side. Her finger traced a different set of marks: faint, almost lost in the clutter of the graph.

“Here,” she said. “The Lantern Whale’s songs. Recordings from the listening shells outside the dome. See? They repeat every cycle, but lately…” She ran her finger along a section where the marks were smeared, drawn out. “Lately they come early. Or late. Or… doubled.”

Mara saw it, then: how the smeared whale-song marks lined up, roughly, with the red door-spikes and the wobbles in the other lines.

“It’s… like something is… nudging,” she said, groping for words. “From… beneath all of it. Like there’s a… a big… thing—”

“Technical vocabulary,” Liora muttered approvingly.

“—turning on its side,” Mara finished. “Or… shifting its weight. Every time it does, the shield… jumps. The doors flicker. The Whale… sings funny. And on the surface…”

She thought of storms, of odd tides, of Captain Thimble muttering about Madam Ocean being in a mood. Of the Museum under her hand, the slow pulse she’d felt.

“...things feel… wrong,” she finished.

Professor Finn’s face had gone very still.

“You think something is pushing against the Veil,” he said. Not mocking. Not dismissive. Testing the words like a wire.

Liora nodded, sharp and sure. “Something deep,” she said. “Deeper than us. Older than the Engine. Old as the first currents.”

“As old as the first tides,” Mara said, the phrase from the prophecy-shell coming uninvited into her mind. “As big as a mountain. Maybe bigger.”

Professor Finn’s hands twitched, as if wanting something to hold on to and finding only trailing tubes.

“There are… things,” he said slowly, “in the deep trenches. Older than cities. Older than stories. We feel them in… omissions. Places the Whale won’t sing over. Areas Madam Ocean steers us gently away from. They rarely move. When they do…” He swallowed. “We get… legends. Of waves where there should be calm. Of holes in the sea.”

“Could one of them be… restless?” Liora asked.

“Or waking up?” Mara said.

Finn shot her a look that was part alarm, part delight, part professional irritation at the narrative escalation. “Let’s not say ‘waking up’ in front of the machinery,” he said hastily. “It gets ideas.”

“But if something that big is shifting,” Liora said, “it would… strain everything. The Veil. The currents. The Door. Your engine.”

“And if the engine tries to… compensate,” Mara said, watching the graph, “it might… open the wrong… bits. Doors. Cracks. Echoes. The tidepool. Maybe the… museum on my side.”

“Museum?” Finn said sharply. “What museum?”

Mara blinked. “On the surface,” she said. “There’s a little… place. Kelp & Curiosities. Captain Thimble Kelp runs it. He—”

She stopped.

Professor Finn’s eyes had gone very, very round.

“Thimble Kelp?” he whispered. “Wooden leg? Beard like a particularly stubborn wave? Smells faintly of stories and guilt?”

“Yes,” Mara said, startled. “You know him?”

Finn made a noise that was probably meant to be a dignified harrumph and came out as a strangled wheeze.

“I might,” he said, “have met him. Once. Or twice. Or sufficiently often to regret it.”

“He’s got a helmet,” Mara blurted. “An old diving helmet. It’s… dripping. All the time. And the water around it feels… like this.”

She gestured at the engine, at the humming air.

Professor Finn closed his eyes briefly, pinched the bridge of his nose again, and muttered something in Pelagorian that the translation shell rendered as, “Of course he does.”

“Oh,” Liora breathed. “Oh. The old stories say there was a first… exchange. A first door. Between here and… a ship. A captain. A promise.”

“And some of us,” Finn said, without opening his eyes, “were foolish enough to help build it.”

Silence settled for a heartbeat, heavy as deep water.

A bubble drifted alongside Mara’s head. Inside it, a tiny, silent thunderstorm flared and faded, oblivious.

“So,” Mara said quietly. “You think… whatever’s pushing… might find its way through there. Through my side. Through his… museum. Through the tidepool.”

“And through here,” Liora said.

“Maybe,” Finn said. “Possibly. Probably. It depends.”

“On what?” Liora asked, a dangerous edge in her voice.

“On whether we can… retune the Engine,” Finn said. “So it hums with whatever is moving, instead of against it. Like… like shifting your weight with the wave, instead of bracing.” He gestured, off-balance. A small spanner fell out of his beard with a clink. “If we resist too hard, we might… crack. If we don’t resist at all…”

“We get nets and storms and drills in the dome,” Liora said flatly. “And a deep thing in our kitchen.”

“And on my side,” Mara said, throat dry, “we get… floods. Weird storms. Shorelines moving. People not understanding why the sea is suddenly… somewhere else.”

They looked at each other, the three of them, standing in the humming, cluttered workshop: the professor with his traitorous engine, the half-sea princess with library ink still faintly staining her fingers, the surface girl with sand in her laces.

Outside, beyond the workshop walls, a flicker of shadow slid over the bubble. The light in the room dimmed for a heartbeat, then brightened again. Somewhere far above, the Lantern Whale sang a note that was almost the right pitch and then, disconcertingly, wasn’t.

“Right,” Professor Finn said briskly, as if a decision had been presented and he’d chosen the option marked Dangerous But Interesting. “We shall have to fix it, then. Before anything terrible happens. Or at least before anything irreversibly terrible happens.”

“You sound very calm about that,” Mara said.

“Oh, I’m not calm at all,” Finn said cheerfully. “I am absolutely terrified. But terror is an excellent motivator. We’ll need data. And patterns. And more waivers.” He pointed at the graph. “You two clearly see things I’ve… been skimming over. So. You will help. Yes?”

Liora’s chin lifted. “Obviously,” she said. “It’s my city.”

Mara’s heart thudded hard enough to make her feel slightly ill. The idea of being anywhere near the engine while it deliberately adjusted itself to a moving, possibly ancient trench-creature was… not comforting.

Then she thought of the tidepool’s glow, the way it had pulled at her. Of Captain Thimble’s warning: Some doors stay open even when we pretend they’re shut. Of her mother rinsing dishes at the sink, oblivious to the fact that the water in the pipes might be listening.

“It’s my… shore,” she said, surprised at the fierce protectiveness in her own voice. “My… upstairs bit. If this goes wrong, it goes wrong there, too. So yes. I’ll help.”

Professor Finn beamed, relief and delight cracking his face into a mess of laugh-lines.

“Splendid!” he said. “Wonderful! Utterly irresponsible, but in the very best way. We’ll recalibrate the Engine. We’ll map the anomalies. We’ll hum along with the Whale until he stops sulking. And you—” He pointed at Mara. “—will go back and forth. Up and down. Through the Door. Bring us tales of strange storms and odd tides and any peculiar behaviour on the part of kettles.”

“Kettles?” Mara said.

“Anything that boils unexpectedly is suspect,” Finn said gravely. “Also any clocks that run backwards, maps that refuse to sit still, or adults who admit they’re afraid.”

“That last one might be the rarest,” Mara said.

“Precisely,” Finn said. “And you—” He turned to Liora. “—will keep your ears on the currents. The Whale. The little shivers in the Veil. Your mother will not thank you. The Council will absolutely not thank you. I shall almost certainly be banned from formal events, which is a mercy. But we may, if we are very careful and slightly lucky, keep everyone from being squashed by something larger than their comprehension.”

“Fine,” Liora said, with the grim satisfaction of someone who has finally been given permission to do exactly what she was going to do anyway. “We’ll need more charts. And access to the Listening Shells. And a way to talk across the Door that isn’t just hoping it opens at convenient times.”

“I can build something,” Finn said immediately, his fingers already twitching, eyes going distant in the way that meant some new, terrible machine had just been born in his head. “A… communicator. A Current-Phone. A Message Mollusc. Something portable. With bells.”

“Nothing with bells,” Liora and Mara said together.

“Fine,” Finn sighed. “No bells. Spoilsports.”

Mara looked back at the wavering lines on the panel.

Behind them, through the clear patch of wall over the Workshop’s door, the dim outline of the Lantern Whale passed again, his flank aglow with gentle lights. One of them, near what might be his head, flared a little brighter as he went by, like a wink.

The graph’s lines wobbled in time with his passing.

Something was pushing against the edges of the world. An ancient engine hummed, trying to keep its promises. A city held its breath, pretending to be calm.

Mara, who had fallen through a door in a tidepool that led downward instead of in, felt a weight settle around her shoulders. It was not light. But it fit, in a strange way, like a backpack she had decided she wanted to carry.

“Okay,” she said again, quietly, into the humming air. “We fix it.”

No one—not Professor Finn with his impossible machine, not Liora with her forbidden library, not Mara with her stubborn, questioning brain—noticed that one, thin, hairline crack had appeared near the very top of the graph, just grazing the dotted threshold and then vanishing.

But the engine felt it.

Far below, in a trench that had forgotten the touch of light, something vast rolled slightly in its sleep and listened, for the first time in a very long time, to the sounds of children planning to change its sea.
