# The Museum That Shouldn’t Exist

Where Mara discovers a shabby seaside museum that is much larger on the inside than on the outside and meets Captain Thimble Kelp.

The storm arrived the way most good storms do: all at once, as if it had been waiting just around the corner for someone to open a door and let it in.

One moment the beach was a stretch of damp sand and scattered shells under a sulking grey sky; the next, the wind decided it had opinions. It came rolling over the sea and along the promenade, snatching at umbrellas, shoving at the swings in the empty playground, and whipping Mara Bright’s hair into her face so thoroughly she could taste salt and loose strands.

She had been walking the long way home on purpose. The short way meant crowded streets and people who asked what a ten-year-old was doing out alone so late, and then you had to look at your shoes and mumble about knowing the way and having a phone and all the other things grown-ups needed to hear to unclench. The long way meant the sea and the rattle of shells and the feeling that the horizon was a question nobody had finished answering yet.

Then the sky cracked open and dropped a sheet of rain onto her head.

Cold needles of water found the gap between her collar and her neck and raced down her spine. The second gust of wind shoved the rain sideways, right into her eyes. Mara squinted, hunched her shoulders around her backpack, and scanned the row of crooked seafront buildings for somewhere that didn’t look like it would collapse if you breathed too hard.

That was when she saw the sign.

It was hanging from a rusted bracket between a fish-and-chips shop and a boarded-up arcade, swinging wildly in the wind like it was trying to escape. The paint was peeling, the wood was warped, and half the letters were the colour of old teeth. It read:

KELP & CURIOSITIES  
MARITIME MUSEUM  
SINCE, MORE OR LESS, 1879

A spray of painted seaweed curled around the words, and a cartoon squid peered over the top with an expression that suggested it had seen things and would very much like to un-see them.

The door underneath the sign was painted the particular flaking green of doors that have given up on being impressive and are just trying to get through the day. It was also slightly crooked, as if it were leaning away from the fish-and-chips shop on one side and the abandoned arcade on the other and didn’t trust either of them.

Mara hesitated for a heartbeat. Then the next blast of wind threw a faceful of rain at her. She ducked her head, grabbed the tarnished brass handle, and slipped inside.

The door closed with a shuddering thump, like something enormous had just exhaled.

For a moment, Mara could hear only the storm raging outside, muffled by the walls, and the echo of her own breathing. The air was cool and full of dust and something else—salt, she realised, and a faint, lingering tang of old rope and rust and that particular dampness that belonged to things that had been wet a long time ago and never entirely forgiven anyone for it.

She pushed her wet hair back from her face and blinked into the dim interior.

From the street, the museum had looked like it couldn’t hold more than a single narrow room: a cramped little corridor with some postcards, maybe, and a rack of souvenir tea towels featuring whales that looked like bloated sausages. Instead, Mara found herself standing at the edge of what felt like a cavern.

The floorboards stretched away in all directions, uneven and dark with age. Glass cases sprouted from them like stalagmites, each one crowded with objects: coils of rope, barnacle-encrusted compasses, bits of coral, carved figureheads with flaking paint and blank, judging eyes. Nets hung from the ceiling in loops and drapes, catching dust instead of fish. Lanterns—actual lanterns, with thick glass and metal frames—hung from chains, glowing with a soft yellow light that clearly wasn’t electricity but also didn’t seem very committed to being fire.

The museum smelled like the underside of a pier and the pages of a very old book had gotten together and agreed to share their best features.

Mara took a cautious step forward. The floorboard under her foot creaked with a long, complaining note, then settled into a steady, almost rhythmic ticking, like someone tutting in the distance.

Her eyes adjusted slowly. The gloom resolved into more detail, and then more, and then she realised that either she was imagining things or she was in a building that was bigger on the inside.

From the outside, Kelp & Curiosities was wedged in like an afterthought between the other shops, barely the width of a bus. From the inside, the room stretched out in a long, meandering hall that bent away into shadows, with alcoves branching off to the left and right. There was a vague awareness at the edges of her vision of more staircases, more galleries above, iron railings, ladders leading… sideways?

At the far end, a staircase spiraled up and then down, though how it managed both at once was not entirely clear.

Mara frowned. She was good with spaces. She liked maps. She could usually tell if a room made sense. This room did not make sense. It did something to her sense of distance, pulling it long like chewing gum.

“Hello?” she called out, because that was what one did, apparently, when one found oneself mysteriously alone in an impossibly large museum that smelled like secrets and salt.

Her voice disappeared into the rafters and came back a moment later, thinner and more dubious.

There was a small bell on the counter by the entrance labelled PRESS IF YOU MUST in wobbly handwriting. Mara considered it. The bell looked like it resented being asked to do anything without proper notice. She decided to let it sulk in peace and wandered further in instead.

To her left, a row of glass cases held ships in bottles. This was ordinary enough, except that these ships did not appear to be entirely aware they were made of wood and glue and were supposed to stay put. Tiny white sails twitched, catching invisible breeze. A miniature wave slapped against a glass bottleneck. One ship, which appeared to be perpetually caught in the moment just before capsizing, creaked ominously and then… didn’t.

Mara leaned close to one of the bottles, fogging the glass with her breath. Inside, a crew of tiny figures scurried about, hauling ropes no thicker than a thread, adjusting sails no bigger than postage stamps. One even seemed to turn, shading its hand over its eyes, to peer back at her.

She jerked back so fast she nearly knocked over the next case. The floor complained loudly. Somewhere deeper in the museum, something metal clanged in protest.

Mara’s heart thumped in her ears. Then she laughed—just once, a small, breathy sound—because she was wet and cold and clearly tired enough to imagine things moving in bottles. Still, she decided, she didn’t need her face quite that close to the glass.

The next case held maps, laid out under glass like flattened, sleeping beasts. Some were yellowed with age; others were dark and stiff, ink faded to a ghostly grey. Waves were drawn with the obsessive detail of someone who loved water but did not entirely trust it. Sea serpents lounged in curling corners. Islands bristled with palm trees and alarming annotations in spidery handwriting: HERE THERE BE WHISPERS. DO NOT PROMISE ANYTHING ON THIS SHORE. ASK THE ROCKS PERMISSION FIRST.

Mara pointed to one coastline that curled like a question mark. “You are not there,” she murmured, because it definitely hadn’t been there when she’d first looked.

The coastline in question had shifted again, subtly, like someone changing their mind halfway through a sentence. A peninsula that had reached bravely out into the sea was now a stub. Two islands had slid closer together and were, in her opinion, definitely plotting something.

She pressed her nose almost to the glass, watching. The lines stayed stubbornly still.

“Fine,” she whispered. “I won’t look away, then.”

Something behind her coughed.

It was the sort of cough that had barnacles on it. It sounded as though it had weathered several storms on its own and come out the other side mildly offended. It also sounded distinctly human.

Mara spun around.

A man stood in the gap between two towering cabinets of sextants and brass tubes. At first glance, he looked like a pile of coats and scarves someone had left there by accident. Then the pile moved, revealing a face tanned to old leather, lined in the way maps were lined, with deep creases at the corners of the eyes from squinting into too many distances.

His hair was wild and grey, sticking out in ruffled waves as if it had tried very hard to be a storm and then, halfway through, forgotten what it was doing. A beard of the same unruly colour clung to his chin, threaded with the occasional glint of copper or brass, as though small bits of hardware had become permanently lost in it.

One leg was solid and booted. The other, from the knee down, was carved from wood the colour of old ship’s hulls, polished smooth by years of use, with tiny waves and ships and sea creatures etched along it. When he shifted, the carvings seemed to ripple.

He leaned on the counter with the familiarity of years, eyeing Mara as if she were an interesting new species of crab that had washed up uninvited.

“Closed,” he said. His voice had the rough-edged quality of someone who had spent a great deal of time shouting over wind and waves and now could only speak in varying degrees of grumble. “Or near enough as makes no odds. Didn’t you see the sign on the door?”

Mara glanced back over her shoulder. From this side, the door was farther away than it had any right to be. The small glass panel set into it did, indeed, have a painted sign hung behind it. It read: CLOSED-ISH.

“Oh,” she said. “I thought that meant ‘probably open if you’re polite about it.’”

The man huffed, which might have been a laugh that hadn’t quite committed. “Hnh. Modern children. Can’t read, but can argue with punctuation.”

“I can read,” Mara said, because this was important. “I just disagreed with your use of the suffix.”

His eyes, which were an unexpected sharp blue, crinkled. “Suffix. Is it. And what, in your expert professional opinion, does ‘ish’ mean, then?”

“It means the boundary is negotiable,” she said promptly. “Like when grown-ups say ‘bedtime-ish’ if it’s the holidays. Or ‘we’ll see’ when they’re going to say no, but they don’t want a discussion yet.”

The man snorted. “Hnh. She’s got you there, Thimble,” he muttered, apparently to himself, and then straightened with the creak of old bones and older wood.

He wiped his hands on his coat, which did not become appreciably cleaner for the effort, and squinted at her. “Right, then. What’ll you be, missy? Lost? Runaway? Wayward school group broke loose and you’re the clever one who found the door?”

“I’m not lost,” Mara said automatically, which was only partially true. She knew where she was on the map; it just felt as though the map had stretched when she wasn’t looking. “I just came in because of the rain. The storm. The unseasonable weather.”

“Unseasonable,” the man repeated, with the air of someone who had been personally affronted by the forecast. “That’s the word for it, right enough. Shouldn’t be blowing like that this time of year. Madam’s in one of her moods.”

Mara glanced instinctively toward the door, as if she might see Madam herself out there, rattling the windows. “Madam?”

He jerked his head toward the general direction of the sea. “Madam Ocean. Great wet temperamental lady out there. Changes her mind every five minutes, then pretends she meant to do that all along. You know her work: tides, storms, swallowing people who annoy her.” He gave her a narrow look. “And leaving others on the doorstep who ought to be in bed.”

“I’m not—” she started, then stopped, because actually she was very likely supposed to be on her way home. “My house isn’t far,” she amended instead. “And my parents know I like to walk by the water. It helps me think.”

“Hnh.” That sound again, somewhere between a grunt and a skeptical gull. “Thinking. Dangerous hobby at your age.” His eyes flicked to the droplets of seawater still clinging to the ends of her hair. “You’ve got the sea on you.”

“It’s raining,” Mara pointed out.

“Not that.” He waved a hand dismissively, then seemed to realise he was being mysterious and frowned at himself. “Never you mind that. What you need is a towel and five minutes to drip somewhere that isn’t my compass drawer. Captain Thimble Kelp, at your service, or at least at your mild inconvenience. Proprietor, curator, reluctant janitor, and general dogsbody of this illustrious establishment. And you are?”

“Mara,” she said. “Mara Bright.”

“Bright, eh?” He tilted his head, as if testing the word. “We shall see about that. Right now you look a bit more like Mara Damp.”

He limped—no, not limped, exactly; he walked with the determined uneven rhythm of someone who had long ago decided that every step was a small argument with gravity and he intended to win them all—around the counter and rummaged in a crate. After a moment of clinking and muttering, he produced a towel that might once have been white but was now the exact unplaceable shade of fabric that has seen too many adventures and not enough detergent.

He handed it to her. “Here. Don’t drip on anything that looks like it might disintegrate. Which is most of it.”

“Thanks,” Mara said, rubbing at her hair. The towel smelled faintly of rope and lemons, as if someone had made a half-hearted attempt to make it pleasant and then been distracted by something involving pulleys.

Captain Thimble watched her for a moment, his head cocked. “So. You wandered in. You could have stayed by the door. Instead you made it all the way to the maps. Hnh. Maps are for people who want to go somewhere they haven’t quite admitted to yet.”

“I like maps,” Mara said, because that felt safer than admitting she liked the feeling of being out of place and yet exactly where she was supposed to be.

“Of course you do. Curious sort, are you?” His eyes twinkled. “The dangerous kind.”

“Is there a safe kind?” Mara asked.

“Not in my experience,” he said cheerfully. “Right. Since you’re in here and I haven’t got the heart to chuck you back into Madam Ocean’s tantrum, you may as well have a look round. Properly, mind. None of this ‘in, out, ooh look a souvenir keyring’ nonsense. You stand still and listen to things. They’ll talk if you give ‘em half a chance.”

“Things talk?” Mara said, delighted despite herself.

“Well, not in words,” Thimble said. “Mostly they mutter. Or vibrate. Or fall on your head at interesting moments. But that’s as may be. This is the Kelp & Curiosities Maritime Museum. Everything in here’s got a story. Some of ‘em are even true.”

“Which ones?” she asked.

He grinned, showing a row of teeth that looked like they’d had disagreements with several hard biscuits and at least one mast. “If I told you that, where’d be the fun?” He gestured broadly with one arm, nearly smacking a hanging net full of glass floats. “Come on, then. I’ll give you the short tour.”

“This is the short tour?” Mara said, glancing again at the impossible length of the hall.

“Short-ish,” he said, with a wink.

He set off between the cases, cane tapping, wooden leg thudding, the rhythm as much a part of the place as the creaking floor. Mara followed, towel draped around her shoulders like a very unimpressive cape.

As they walked, he talked—rambling, sideways narratives that looped and doubled back on themselves like tangled rigging.

“This here,” he said, stopping by a glass case in which a length of rope lay coiled, fatter than Mara’s arm and stiff with age, “is a piece of the Great Rope of Arelia. Strongest rope ever woven. They say it was spun from the hair of the Storm Sisters, three witches who lived on a cliff and argued with thunder. Anchored the biggest ship ever built. Ship still sank, mind you. Turns out you can’t tie a knot in a promise.”

Mara peered closer. The rope did look like it might have been spun from something once alive; tiny threads of silver glinted in the rough fibres, catching the lantern light.

“Why did the ship sink?” she asked.

“Captain promised too much,” Thimble said, almost absently. “Sea remembers.” He shook himself slightly and moved on.

They passed a collection of figureheads: women with hair that had once been bright gold or red now flaked down to muted streaks, men with proud carved chins, beasts with open mouths frozen mid-roar. One mermaid’s eyes were inlaid with tiny chips of what might have been mother-of-pearl; they seemed wet.

“Do they watch people?” Mara asked, not entirely joking.

“Only the liars,” Thimble said. “So you’re safe if you keep your fibs small and your truths big.”

They turned a corner that shouldn’t have existed, given the shape of the building as seen from outside. The air grew cooler, and the smell of salt was stronger here, less memory and more presence. The floor felt not just old but… layered, as if many floors had been piled on top of each other until no one could remember where the first one had started.

Mara slowed. “How… big is this place?” she asked, unable to keep the wonder out of her voice.

“Oh, you know,” Thimble said vaguely. “Big enough to hold what it needs to. Small enough the council doesn’t notice.” He squinted up at the ceiling, where several rafters were doing their best impression of a ship’s hull turned inside out. “Buildings expand if you put enough stories in ‘em. Never seen a bookshelf that stopped at the wall, have you? They just keep going up in your head. Same principle. Now, over here we’ve got the starboard annex—”

“Starboard?” Mara said. “Isn’t that… ships?”

“Everything’s ships if you argue hard enough,” Thimble said. “Walls, floors, people. Some just haven’t grown their sails yet.”

The starboard annex contained, among other things, a brass instrument taller than Mara, bristling with levers and lenses and little glass bulbs that glowed with an eerie blue light. It hummed faintly, the sound prickling against her teeth. A small hand-painted sign at its base read: EXPERIMENTAL TIDE CALCULATOR (DO NOT ASK IT ABOUT THURSDAYS).

“What happens on Thursdays?” Mara asked.

“Nothing you need to see before you’ve had algebra,” Thimble said firmly.

They wandered on. The storm outside faded into background growls and occasional flashes of light through high, grimy windows. Time stretched, loosening at the edges. Mara lost track of how long they’d been walking. It could have been ten minutes; it could have been an hour. The museum felt like the sort of place where clocks forgot their jobs.

Every now and then, Thimble glanced sideways at her, as if checking she was still there and hadn’t dissolved into a puddle of seawater and curiosity.

“You’re a quiet one,” he observed eventually. “Most children I get in here, they’re all fingers and noise. Pick up everything, ask if it’s haunted, then demand a gift shop. You look instead. You listen.”

Mara shrugged, uncomfortable under the attention. “People talk a lot,” she said. “But they don’t always say anything.”

He barked a laugh. “Oh, you’re trouble. I can tell.”

They came at last to a corner of the museum that was less well lit. The nearest lantern flickered uncertainly, casting wavering shadows. Here, things were piled more haphazardly: crates stacked on crates, tarps thrown over bulky shapes, barrels with stencilled words too faded to read. It felt less like an exhibition and more like the attic of someone with a very specific obsession.

Mara’s gaze snagged on a shape half-hidden behind a crate of cracked glass buoys.

A battered diving helmet sat on a low wooden stand. It was the old-fashioned kind she’d seen in pictures in books: big and round, made of dulled copper, with thick glass portholes like watchful eyes. Tubes emerged from the sides, snaking down toward a coil of hose on the floor. Bolts ringed the neck where it would fasten to a suit. A faint green tinge crawled over the metal where sea and oxygen had quarrelled.

All of that was ordinary enough, for certain values of ordinary.

The not-ordinary thing was that water was dripping from it.

A slow bead of clear seawater formed at the rim of the front porthole, swelled, and then dropped with a soft pat onto the warped floorboards below. Another drop was already forming behind it, patient and inevitable, as if there were an entire ocean contained inside the helmet and it had decided to leak.

Mara stepped closer, captivated. The air around the helmet felt cooler, damp but not unpleasantly so, like the breath of a cave mouth in summer. Her skin prickled with goosebumps that had nothing to do with the damp towel now hanging forgotten over her arm.

“Careful,” Thimble said sharply. His cane came down with a thud that had more to it than wood on wood, as if it were a warning to the floor as well as to her.

“It’s dripping,” Mara said, unable to keep the wonder out of her voice. “Why is it still wet?”

Thimble’s face did something complicated. The lines at the corners of his mouth deepened. His eyes, which had been full of mockery and half-amusement, shuttered slightly, as though he were pulling a curtain behind them.

“Old leak,” he said, too casually. “Pipes in this place have a mind of their own. Get condensation, that’s all. Nothing to bother about.”

He was lying.

Mara knew the taste of lies. Adults lied the way they put on coats: casually, because the weather demanded it. Some lies were big and clumsy, like “It won’t hurt” before injections, or “We’ll talk about it later,” which translated to “We will never talk about this again and I hope you forget.” Some were small and papery, like “We’ll see.”

This lie felt different. It felt heavy, weighted down with something unsaid. It sat between them like another person.

“If it’s just condensation,” she said, picking her words with careful innocence, “why does it smell like the sea?”

She didn’t mean the general salt-and-must of the museum. There was, around the helmet, a different smell: cold and deep and clean, the scent of water far below where the waves broke, where things were old and slow and listening.

Thimble’s hand tightened on his cane. For a moment, he looked not like an old man in a shabby coat in a too-big museum, but like someone who had once stood on a deck in the middle of an unending night, listening for something in the swell of the waves and hoping it wouldn’t answer.

“Some doors,” he said, after a too-long pause, “stay open even when we pretend they’re shut.”

The words hung in the air, heavier than they should have been.

Mara tore her gaze away from the helmet to stare at him. “Doors?” she echoed.

“Mm.” He blew out his cheeks, as if annoyed with himself for saying even that much. “Places you can go. Places that can come to you. Holes worn through in the world, like in an old net. You patch ’em as best you can, but the sea…” He trailed off, shook his head. “The sea doesn’t always agree with your idea of a good patch.”

“That’s not a very reassuring answer,” Mara said quietly.

“Wasn’t trying to reassure you,” Thimble said. “Reassurance’s how people get themselves eaten by metaphors.” He opened his mouth as if to add something, then thought better of it. “Anyway. The helmet’s an old piece. Relic. Leftover. Not for climbing into. Not for poking. Not for—” He caught the look in her eyes and pointed a finger at her. “Not for curious children to put on their heads and go exploring. Clear?”

Mara bristled, torn between indignation and the thrill of being seen through so completely. “I wasn’t going to—”

“You were absolutely going to,” he said. “Could see it in your shoulders. All set to lean just that bit too far. You’ve got that look.” His gaze softened, just for a moment. “The one that asks ‘What if?’ louder than it asks ‘Should I?’.”

He turned away abruptly, as if annoyed with his own softness. “Come on. Enough of that,” he said more briskly. “There’s other things to see that don’t drip or sulk or tempt you into trouble.”

He moved off, but Mara lingered for one more heartbeat, drawn to the slow, steady drip. She watched another bead of water form and fall. The tiny splash sounded too loud in her ears.

The floor beneath the helmet was damp, darker than the surrounding boards. She could see faint salt crystals blossoming where drops had evaporated, pale and sharp-edged. A small puddle had collected in a shallow dip; the surface trembled, as if something far away had moved.

She swallowed and turned to follow Thimble.

They took another path now, looping back—or maybe deeper in; distance was unreliable here—past a display of bottles filled with sand from beaches all over the world. Each was labelled in careful ink: PORT SAINT-LOUIS, FINE AS FLOUR; SKAGERRAK, SHARP AS TEETH; NARROW STRAND, DO NOT OPEN.

“Never open bottled sand,” Thimble advised. “You think you’re tipping out a nice little souvenir, next thing you know you’re ankle-deep in someone else’s shoreline and arguing with their crabs.”

Mara murmured something that might have been agreement. Her thoughts were still back with the dripping helmet and the idea of doors that didn’t quite close.

They reached a long wall lined with framed photographs: ships in harbours, the sea in all its disguises, people in stiff old clothes squinting into the sun. Thimble paused before one of them: a black-and-white image of a younger version of himself standing on the deck of a ship, hair only marginally less wild, arm slung around another sailor whose face had been damaged by time or by something else—the features were blurred, the outline smudged.

He looked at it only briefly, then moved on without comment. Mara filed that away in the part of her mind labelled Things Adults Don’t Say Because They Think You’ll Break.

After a while, the echo of her own footsteps started to sound strange to her, as if there were someone walking almost in time with her, half a beat out of sync. The floor creaked and settled. Somewhere, something that might have been a clock chimed a time that didn’t correspond to anything on her watch.

They passed a display of shells arranged in spirals and lines. As they drew near, Mara could swear she heard a faint susurrus, like distant whispers. When she leaned closer to a large conch, the sound sharpened into something almost like words in a language she didn’t know but nearly understood.

She straightened quickly. “Do you ever feel like the building is… listening?” she asked.

Thimble’s mouth twitched. “Building?” he said. “Oh, no. This isn’t a building. This is a story that got too big to stay on a shelf, so it grew walls to keep the rain out.” He tapped his cane twice on the floorboards. The wood answered with a hollow, resonant thump. “She creaks when the tide changes,” he added, almost fondly. “Makes her own weather sometimes, too, when Madam gets moody.”

As if on cue, a low groan sounded under their feet, followed by a ripple of movement that ran through the floor like a breath. Mara stumbled, catching herself on the edge of a glass case. The boards felt, for a moment, less like wood and more like ribs.

“Did you feel that?” she whispered.

“Old place,” Thimble said lightly, though his gaze flicked down, measuring. “Settling. Remembering. Might be the storm outside nudging her nerves. She doesn’t entirely approve of gales. Says they’re show-offs.”

Mara wasn’t satisfied with that answer. The sensation had been unmistakable: the slow rise and fall underfoot, as if something enormous and patient lay beneath the museum, sleeping. For a second, the thought of the ocean—not the polite fringe of waves on the beach, but the deep, dark weight of it far out—rose in her mind, and she felt very small.

She wandered toward a patch of wall between two display cases. It was an unremarkable section of wooden planking, painted the same tired off-white as the other walls, scuffed where people had brushed past over the years. But her skin prickled as she neared it.

Mara reached out, almost without thinking, and laid her palm flat against the wood.

The museum’s noises dwindled. The creak of boards, the dim mutter of Thimble talking about something behind her, the distant rumble of the storm—all of it faded to a muffled hush, as if she had put her head underwater.

Beneath her hand, the wall was cool. Not the cool of shade or unheated buildings, but a deeper cold, the kind that seeped into rocks at the bottom of the sea. The wood, which should have felt solid and unchanging, seemed to flex, infinitesimally, against her skin.

Thump.

It was slow, so slow that if she hadn’t been concentrating she might have missed it entirely. A pressure, a subtle outward push, like a pulse.

Thump.

Her own heartbeat answered, quick and startled. The wall’s pulse remained unhurried, immense, indifferent.

“That’s not—” she began, her voice strange in her own ears, and then stopped, because she didn’t know how to finish that sentence. That’s not possible? That’s not wood? That’s not just a building?

From somewhere behind her: “Mara? Best not touch—”

She pressed her hand more firmly against the wall. The cold deepened, but it wasn’t unfriendly. It reminded her of being a small child standing ankle-deep in the surf, feeling the pull of the tide around her feet, the sense that something enormous and restless was moving beneath the surface, paying no attention to her and yet holding her up.

Images flooded her mind, not sharp enough to call visions, but more than imagination: green light filtering down through water; shapes drifting in the blue; the glimmer of something far below, like a city made of lanterns; silence, and then a sound like a whale’s call stretched into forever.

Her breath hitched. Her fingers tingled. For a heartbeat—hers or the wall’s, she couldn’t tell—the world narrowed to that point of contact.

“Enough,” Thimble said, closer now, voice low but edged. A warm, calloused hand closed gently but firmly around her wrist, lifting her palm away from the wood.

The connection broke with a sensation like surf withdrawing from sand. The noises of the museum rushed back in: the creak, the distant rattle, the hum of some unseen machine complaining about the damp. Mara swayed, trying to reorient herself.

She looked up at Thimble. For the first time since she’d met him, he looked genuinely unsettled.

“You felt that,” she said, more statement than question.

He released her wrist slowly. His fingers left faint impressions in her skin, like the memory of a rope.

“Some stories,” he said, choosing his words with care, “are meant to rock you to sleep. Some are meant to wake you up. And some aren’t meant for children who ought to be home by supper.”

“That wall is—” She groped for the right word and fell, inevitably, on the obvious. “—alive.”

He snorted, but there was no real humour in it. “Everything’s alive if you look close enough. Stones. Seas. Maps. Old men.” He glanced at the wall, then away again, as if looking at it too long hurt his eyes. “This place sits where it sits,” he went on. “Here and… not entirely here. There are currents in wood as well as water. Sometimes they… syncopate.”

“Syncopate,” Mara repeated softly. She knew the word from music lessons she’d once had and abandoned, when the piano teacher had become far more interested in telling Mara to sit up straight than in the strange stormy piece Mara had wanted to learn. Syncopation: the stress on the weak beat, the shift where you didn’t expect it.

She thought of tides. Of doors that refused to stay shut.

“I thought this was just a museum,” she said, but what she really meant was, I thought this was solid.

Captain Thimble Kelp regarded her with those sharp blue eyes that had seen too much sea and not enough sleep.

“Nothing is ‘just’ anything,” he said. “Not if you stare at it long enough. This place…” He gestured vaguely around them. “This place remembers more than it ought to. Some of it’s mine. Some of it’s Madam’s. Some of it belongs to other folk. Folk who live…” He inclined his head slightly toward the wall she’d touched, toward the hidden world beneath the waves. “…elsewhere.”

Mara’s heart thumped too fast. Images from that strange moment of contact eddied at the edge of her mind: glimmers of light, slow shadows in deep water.

“You mean—” she began.

“I mean,” he interrupted briskly, “that it’s late. And you’re wet. And if I let you stay any longer, you’ll sink roots into my floor and Madam Ocean will knock a hole in the foundations to come see what I’m doing with her favourite sort of creature.”

“Favourite sort?” Mara said.

“Curious,” he said simply. “She likes the ones who lean over edges.” He nodded toward the door, which was now, without warning, only a short distance away. Mara blinked. She was certain they had been deeper inside the museum a moment before, but the entrance was right there: the glass panel, the CLOSED-ISH sign, the brass handle beaded with condensation from the storm outside.

“How did we get…?” she started.

“Short cut,” Thimble said, with the air of someone who wasn’t going to explain how he’d folded space because then he’d have to draw diagrams and he’d never get his tea. “Museum knows when to take pity on small drippy humans. Off you go, Miss Bright. Come back when it’s not trying to rain sideways and your parents won’t send the coastguard out after you.”

Mara stood still. The towel felt heavy on her shoulders. Her palm still tingled faintly where it had touched the wall. There were a thousand questions pressing against her teeth, jostling to be first: What did you mean, ‘other folk’? What’s behind the wall? Why is the helmet dripping? Have you been there? Can I go?

Instead, she heard herself say, “Is it all real?”

Thimble paused with his hand on the back of a chair that definitely hadn’t been there before. His shoulders slumped a fraction, as if someone had let air out of them.

“Depends what you mean by real,” he said at last. “Is the tide real, if you only believe in land? Are stories real, if you can’t trip over them? You’ve seen ships in bottles that remember how to move. Maps that forget to stay put. A wall that pulses like a heart. You tell me.”

“I…” Mara swallowed. “I don’t know yet.”

He smiled, a quick sideways thing that looked almost shy. “Good answer. The moment you’re sure of everything, you stop seeing the bits that don’t fit. And those are the important bits.”

“Will you be here tomorrow?” she asked, surprising herself.

“Unless Madam decides to relocate the whole enterprise to the bottom of the bay out of spite,” he said. “I open at ten-ish. Close at five-ish. Same rules as the tide: entirely unpredictable unless you pay attention.” He tilted his head. “You’ll come back?”

It wasn’t a demand. It wasn’t even really a question. It was curiosity, offered like a rope.

“Yes,” Mara said. The word felt like a promise—not made to him, or even to herself, but to the strange deep something that had pulsed beneath her hand. “I’ll come back.”

He nodded, as if this confirmed some private theory. “Mind your step on the way out,” he said. “She’s a bit twitchy when the weather’s like this.”

Mara nodded and reached for the doorknob. As her fingers closed around the cold metal, she hesitated and glanced back one last time.

The museum stretched away into dimness; the lanterns swayed gently, as though under the influence of some current. The dripping helmet sat in its shadowed corner, just visible between two stacks of crates. Even from here, she could see a tiny flicker of reflected light in its glass, like the suggestion of something moving behind it.

Captain Thimble Kelp stood beside the counter, one hand resting on the worn wood, his wooden leg bent just so, his storm-tousled hair creating its own minor weather system around his head. He lifted a hand in a small salute.

Outside, the storm roared.

Mara opened the door. Wind slapped her in the face, wet and wild. For a moment the world was all grey and noise. She stepped out, pulling the door shut behind her with both hands.

It shut with that same deep, shuddering thump, like a heart closing around a secret.

She stood on the slick pavement, immediately soaked again, hair plastered to her cheeks, shoes squelching. The promenade was almost empty; a single gull hunched on a lamppost, looking miserable. The fish-and-chips shop’s neon sign buzzed and flickered. The boarded-up arcade sighed.

Mara turned to look at the museum. From the outside, it was as it had been: a narrow little building squeezed between its neighbours, leaning slightly sideways. The sign creaked in the wind. The windows were small and murky. Light glowed faintly behind the glass, but it was the sort of light that belonged to one tired bulb, not to a cavern full of lanterns and moving maps.

She reached out and touched the peeling green paint of the door. It was just a door. Chilly under her fingers, rain-slick, but no pulse.

Still, her own heart beat a little faster, echoing something she had felt inside.

She glanced down. At the edge of her thumbnail, a tiny white salt crystal clung, glittering faintly.

Mara smiled, a little unevenly, and curled her hand into a fist around it.

The storm tugged at her, urging her home. She set off along the promenade, the wind at her back, the taste of salt on her lips, and the knowledge that somewhere, just a few feet and a world away, there was a shabby seaside museum with a heartbeat in its walls and a door that didn’t entirely know the meaning of closed.

Behind her, in the Kelp & Curiosities Maritime Museum, water dripped—steadily, patiently—from a battered diving helmet, forming a growing puddle on the floor. And far beneath the town, beneath the sand and rock and restless waves, something vast and luminous turned in its sleep at the faint touch of a child’s hand against its distant, wooden skin.
