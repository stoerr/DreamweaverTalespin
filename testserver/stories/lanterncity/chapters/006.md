# The Lantern in the Trench

Where Mara, Liora, and Professor Finn travel to the ocean trench to seek the wisdom of the Lantern Whale and witness the damage that fear has done.

The submersible looked, in Mara’s considered opinion, like someone had persuaded an oversized soap bubble to swallow a mechanical crab and then strapped umbrellas to the result.

It waited for them in one of Pelagoria’s lower harbours, cradled in a pocket of air that bulged out from the city’s main dome like a blister. The harbour itself was a shallow bowl hollowed into the inner wall, lined with smooth, worn stone. Water lapped gently at the lower edge, where the air ended and the true sea began, its surface forming a soft, shimmering curve.

The bubble-boat—because that was what Professor Finn resolutely called it—rocked faintly on an invisible axis, half in air, half nestled against the water’s skin. Its main body was a clear, rounded sphere big enough to hold three or four people who were willing to be on very familiar terms with each other. Around its waist, a belt of metal ribs clung like a girdle, studded with control levers and a few worrying patches of hand-soldered something.

From the sides sprouted fins: wide, fan-like constructs made of layered, translucent material that reminded Mara of the undersides of certain jellyfish. At the rear, a larger pair stretched and flexed, stirring the water with slow, experimental strokes. Every time the fins moved, the boat emitted a faint, reedy wheeze, like an asthmatic accordion.

A hatch set into the top of the bubble stood open. Professor Finn crouched beside it on a little platform, fussing with a bundle of tubes that ran from the bubble’s belt into a nest of valves and dials. His patched diving suit was sealed this time, helmet tucked under one arm, its glass faceplate fogged around the edges with his breath.

Behind them, Queen Nerissa’s presence made the air in the harbour feel thinner.

She stood at the edge of the dry stone, robes falling around her in measured ripples, coral crown catching the filtered light. A cluster of guards ringed her at a respectful distance, tridents held upright, their pale blades almost delicate against the curve of water behind.

“This is folly,” she said, and made it sound like a simple fact, not an argument. “Spectacular folly.”

“Yes,” Professor Finn said cheerfully, not looking up. “But productive folly, we hope. And it’s the only way to talk to him properly.”

Nerissa’s gaze slid to her daughter.

Liora stood straight, bare feet planted firmly on the damp stone, hands clasped loosely behind her back in an attempt at appearing less like she was about to explode with restless energy. Her scales caught the light along her arms. Her expression said: I know you are the Queen. I also know you are not going to stop me.

“You touch nothing that glows without asking,” Nerissa said to her, tone clipped. “You do not stray from the vessel. You will listen when Professor Finn says we must rise. You will not—”

“—wake anything large,” Liora finished, rolling her eyes. “Yes, Mother. You’ve said.”

“I will say it as often as I must to make the point bed in,” Nerissa snapped, then caught herself, smoothing her voice. “Liora. This is not a story. There are things in the deep you have never seen and should never wish to. The Whale is not the only ancient there. And even he is not always… safe.”

“I know,” Liora said, and for a second some of the brittle humour dropped from her voice. “But he’s hurting. I can hear it in his songs. If our Veil is part of that hurt, don’t we owe it to him to listen?”

Nerissa’s jaw worked. The muscles in her throat shifted, betraying words unsaid. Then she lifted a hand and brushed a stray tendril of hair back from Liora’s cheek, fingers lingering for a heartbeat against the line of faint scales.

“If he calls,” she said softly, “answer. If he turns away, you do the same. You are not there to fix him. You are there to learn.”

Liora nodded, serious now. “Yes, Mother.”

Nerissa’s gaze moved to Mara.

Mara fought the urge to shuffle her feet. She had the unnerving sensation of standing in the beam of an extremely powerful moral lighthouse.

“You,” Nerissa said, “owe me three explanations, two apologies, and at least one report.”

“I… can start on the report?” Mara offered.

One corner of the Queen’s mouth twitched, very slightly. “Finn will see that you are briefed on the necessary safety charms,” she said. “You will not attempt to leave the vessel. You will not attempt to touch the Whale’s lanterns. You will not attempt to… negotiate with anything.”

“Absolutely not,” Mara said, because even her curiosity flinched at the idea of trying to bargain with something the size of a small mountain.

“And Mara,” Nerissa said, voice dropping low enough that the guards politely pretended not to hear. “If you see anything that feels… wrong… in a way you do not have words for yet, you tell me. Whether my Council thinks it relevant or not.”

Mara blinked. “You… want my… opinion?”

“I want your eyes,” Nerissa said. “They have not learned yet how to look away.”

She hesitated, then added, “My people’s fear weighs on this city like extra water. Sometimes I cannot tell where it has bent my sight. You have not been here long enough to share our… blind spots.”

Mara thought of how adults on the surface sometimes asked children what they thought and then smiled indulgently and went on ignoring them. This sounded different. Less like performance, more like need.

“I’ll tell you,” she said. “If I… notice anything. Even if I can’t explain it very well.”

“Imperfect words are better than silence,” Nerissa said. “Go.”

She stepped back as Professor Finn straightened, satisfied with whatever last-minute tinkering he’d inflicted on the bubble-boat.

“All right!” he said, helmet under one arm, eyes bright. “Boat’s sealed, fins are flapping, Voice Modulator is—” He paused, frowned at a small panel, and hit it with the heel of his hand. A faint crackle answered. “—mostly fine. Everyone in.”

Liora hopped lightly onto the little platform and swung herself down through the open hatch with an ease that made Mara’s knees hurt just to watch. Mara, less water-bred and more accustomed to things like stairs, clambered after, accepting Professor Finn’s steadying hand as she lowered herself into the bubble.

The inside of the submersible was as cramped as it had looked. A curved bench circled the inner wall, padded in patches, scuffed in others. In the centre, a post rose up from the floor, sprouting levers and a wheel and what appeared to be an entire cluster of tiny, colour-coded valves with labels like DO NOT TOUCH and REALLY DO NOT TOUCH in Professorial scrawl.

The transparency of the walls made Mara’s stomach flip. There was something unnerving about being able to see, all at once, the harbour stone beneath their bubble, the curved air–water boundary ahead, and the darkening water beyond that.

Professor Finn followed them in, slightly less gracefully, and pulled the hatch shut over his head. A series of bolts snicked into place with a snug, final sound.

“Breathing?” he asked.

Mara inhaled experimentally. The air was cool and a bit damp, faintly tinged with oil and metal and the indefinable smell of enclosed spaces.

“Breathing,” she confirmed.

“Annoyed?” Liora added.

“Moderately,” Mara said.

“Excellent,” Finn said. “Baseline conditions established.”

He twisted a dial. Something thumped quietly beneath their feet. The bubble rocked. The fan-fins outside flexed and spread, their shadows rippling over the harbour floor.

Mara swallowed as the world outside began to move.

The stone lip of the harbour slipped upward in her field of view; the curving interface of water rushed closer. For a disorienting instant, it was as if they were plunging toward a mirror of themselves.

Then the bubble-boat met the water.

There was no splash. Instead, it was like being wrapped in a cool, firm skin. The thin shell of air clung to them inside their clear globe; outside, the world blurred for a second as they passed through. The sounds of the harbour—voices, footsteps, the distant rumble of machinery—muffled abruptly, replaced by a quieter, thicker silence, punctuated only by the faint hum of the boat’s mechanisms.

The light changed.

Pelagoria’s lower dome fell away above them, its graceful architecture shrinking as they descended. The city lights turned from individual glows to a hazy, luminous smear, like a bioluminescent galaxy pinned to the underside of the ocean’s skin.

Beyond that halo, blue deepened around them. Not the friendly, airy blue of sunny days, but a denser, cooler shade. It thickened with every meter they dropped, turning from turquoise to indigo, from indigo toward ink.

The fan-fins paddled with steady, measured strokes. Their faint, breathy wheeze filled the bubble like nervous whistling.

“Professor?” the boat said in a small, squeaky voice.

Mara jerked. “Did… it… just…?”

“Oh, yes,” Finn said, flicking a switch. “Meet Bubbles.”

“Please don’t call me that,” the boat said, in tones of weary resignation. “It is not dignified.”

“We are descending, Bubbles,” Finn told it. “Do let me know when the pressure makes you uncomfortable.”

“I am already uncomfortable,” Bubbles said. “You have wedged three people and a week’s worth of poor decisions inside me and are now taking me somewhere dark.”

“It complains,” Finn explained to Mara. “It helps regulate the air. If it stops complaining, tell me immediately. It will mean something has gone terribly wrong.”

“Good to know,” Mara said faintly.

Outside, the world dimmed further. The last of Pelagoria’s light receded behind them, leaving only a soft, general glow above. The water itself grew darker, not because it wasn’t there before, but because Mara’s eyes, limited surface things that they were, failed to parse its depth.

They passed through layers.

Here, a zone of hazy particulates that glimmered briefly in the sub’s own faint light, like dust motes in a sunbeam. There, a band of clearer water, colder, that made the bubble’s skin creak audibly. The pressure gauge—a cluster of concentric circles painted on the inner wall, with a little arrow that wobbled as they descended—began to creep upward.

“Eeeeee,” Bubbles said.

“That is not a helpful diagnostic noise,” Finn said. “Use your words.”

“The outside is getting… heavy,” Bubbles said. “Squashy. I do not like squashy.”

“You’re holding perfectly,” Finn said, tapping a reassuring gauge. “Structural integrity is at ninety-eight percent.”

“What happened to the other two percent?” Bubbles demanded, offended.

“Existential dread,” Finn said promptly. “Quite normal at this depth.”

“The light’s going,” Liora murmured, leaning forward, palms pressed to the inside of the bubble. Her hair floated around her head, framing her face in dark cloud. “Look.”

Mara looked.

Above them, the vague brightness that marked Pelagoria’s region thinned, scattered by distance and particulate. Below, a different kind of darkness gathered: not empty, but full. Full of water, of pressure, of the endless slow movements of things that never saw the sun.

Shapes appeared at the edge of perception.

A long, pale form slipping past: a fish with too many teeth and no interest in their little bubble. A cluster of tiny specks blinking on and off, like fragments of a broken constellation drifting on an unseen current. Once, far to the right, something enormous and indistinct rolled in the gloom and vanished, leaving behind a swirl of silt that drifted lazily for long minutes.

The deeper they went, the more vivid the small lights became. Strange jellyfish pulsed with internal phosphorescence, trailing threads that glowed faintly green. Some cratered creature on the seafloor flexed, its rim studded with tiny lanterns that flickered in irritation at the disturbance.

“How deep are we?” Mara asked, trying not to press too hard against the glass. It felt like leaning on the skin of something breathing.

“Past the city’s comfort,” Finn said. “Beyond the reach of most of our swimmers. Into the long-fall region. The trench itself is… further yet.”

“Further?” Bubbles squeaked. “We are already at ‘absolutely not recommended.’ The gauge says so. Look.”

The arrow on the pressure dial trembled near a band that Finn had, unhelpfully, marked with a little skull and a note that read: PROBABLY FINE, BUT.

“We’re still within your design limits,” Finn said. “I gave you an extra margin. For personality.”

“I do not want a personality,” Bubbles said. “I want a nice, shallow puddle and perhaps a quiet life as a decorative aquarium.”

Liora made a small sound that might, in less terrifying circumstances, have been a laugh.

“Look,” she said again, but this time there was awe threaded through the single word.

Ahead, the darkness changed quality.

It was subtle at first: a kind of faint, velvety softening in one direction. Then, as the bubble-boat paddled onward, small, floating points of light began to appear at the edge of the gloom.

They were different from the scattered glows they’d been seeing. These were larger, more deliberate, spaced with intention. Each one burned a soft, milky white with a faint halo of other colours—pale blue, sea-green, the occasional soft rose.

They hung in the water in loosely parallel lines, curving gently with some vast geometry Mara couldn’t yet see. More and more of them appeared as they moved closer, until an entire wall of twinkling lights floated in front of them, like a Milky Way turned sideways and submerged.

“Lanterns,” Liora whispered. There was reverence in her voice. “His lanterns.”

The bubble-boat slowed, fins folding in with a soft hiss. Finn adjusted a lever, eyes narrowing. He said something low and wordless that was half request, half greeting.

Something enormous moved in the dark.

It wasn’t a sudden motion. It was more like the shifting of a hillside, the slow, almost imperceptible adjustment of something that had spent a very long time not needing to go anywhere.

The lines of lights swung, subtly, as if attached to the flanks of a vast, turning body.

Then he was there.

The Lantern Whale slid into their limited field of view like an eclipse.

Mara had seen whales in pictures. She had a vague idea of their size. None of that had prepared her for this.

He was colossal.

Not just in length—though that was impressive enough, his body stretching away into the dimness both ahead of and behind their bubble—but in presence. He filled the water around them with slow, massive certainty. His head was broad, his jawline curved, his mouth a long, shadowed line. His skin was a dark, almost black blue-grey that drank in what little light reached it, the ridges and scars along his flanks smoothed by time.

Down his sides, along the gentle arc of his back and the thick base of his tail, ran rows of lanterns.

They weren’t actual lanterns, of course. They were living tissues, bioluminescent organs embedded in his skin, each one a gently glowing node. From a distance, they looked like strings of floating orbs half-wrapped around a mountain. Up close, Mara could see that each had a slightly different hue and pulse, like individual thoughts.

His eye was the size of Mara’s outstretched arm from fingertip to shoulder. It glimmered as it turned toward them, a deep, dark iris ringed with a pale blue halo. For a moment, their little bubble-boat was reflected there, a tiny, trembling spark in the dark mirror.

Mara’s breath caught.

“Hello,” she whispered, entirely without meaning to. The word fogged the inner surface of the bubble in front of her.

The Whale’s presence pressed against her mind not as sound, not quite as thought, but as a slow, enveloping awareness: like lying very still under blankets and feeling the house breathe. There was weight in it. Age. The patient, long-viewing eye of something that had watched continents crack and currents learn their routes.

Then, through the translation shell that Nerissa had anchored around Mara’s hearing, through a filter of distorting water and complicated physics and Finn’s humming machinery, came a sound.

It started so low she felt it more than heard it.

A deep, thrumming note rolled through the water, through the bubble, through her bones. It climbed, slowly, carrying harmonics with it like trailing fish, until it hovered just at the edge of her hearing. There, it broke into a pattern: a sequence of rises and falls, long and short, threaded with subtle changes in timbre.

“They are small,” said the translation net in Mara’s mind, draping meaning over the whale-song like a subtitle. The voice it chose—because somehow it had to choose a voice for something this vast—was deep and resonant, but slowed, as if each word took time to arrive.

“Hello, old friend,” Professor Finn said quietly, pressing his palm against the inside of the bubble.

The Whale’s lanterns nearest them brightened slightly, then dimmed, like an eye narrowing in recognition.

“Maker of humming,” the translated voice said. “Child of coral and clever fingers. You have brought… others.”

The lanterns along his flank rippled in a slow wave. Two in particular near the midline flared and dimmed in a pattern.

“Name-lights,” Liora whispered. “He’s asking who we are.”

She pressed her own hand to the bubble, a handspan from Finn’s. The Whale’s nearest lanterns bloomed in a matching configuration, lines of light connecting them briefly like a diagram.

“Liora of the Glass Tides,” the net interpreted. “Half-swimmer. Half-walker. Quick tongue. Sharp ears.”

Liora flushed. “He remembers me,” she whispered, eyes wide.

Mara, heart thumping, reached out her own hand.

The Whale’s gaze—if that’s what it was, that steady, unsettling sense of attention—rested on her. For a moment she felt impossibly small, a dot on a dot on the skin of a bubble hanging in his field of view.

Then the lights near his head kindled, flickering in a new pattern.

“Sky-breath,” the voice said. “Door-fallen. Bright-eyes. Question-heart.”

Mara’s throat tightened.

“I’m… Mara,” she said, voice small in the bubble. “From… above. From… the shore.”

The Whale’s lanterns glowed, a soft, almost amused pulse.

“Above,” he repeated, the concept pulled slowly through the heavy medium of his understanding. “The up-water. The place of hard air and sharp sun. I remember. Long ago.”

His great body shifted, muscles like underwater hills rolling under his skin. The water around them thickened with the slow turbulence.

“You come… deep,” he said. “Deeper than your kind should. You come with the hum-maker and the half-child. You come on the Queen’s thread. Why?”

Liora shot a glance at Finn. Finn nodded slightly, encouraging.

“We think something is wrong with the Veil,” Liora said. “With… your… paths.”

She fumbled, searching for words that would carry. “The shield that makes us… hidden. The humming shell around Pelagoria. We think… it is hurting you.”

The Whale’s lanterns dimmed.

A silence rolled out from him like a tide withdrawing.

“When the hum began,” he said, after a long beat, “I learned its rhythm. I bent my songs around it. The small ones”—a flicker of light near his tail, perhaps indicating fish, or currents, or both—“learned its edges. It was… strange. But not… pain.”

He rolled slightly, exposing more of his flank. Some of the lanterns there were not smooth. A few glowed irregularly, edges blurred. One patch of skin was paler, marred by a faint, jagged line where no light shone.

“Then the hum… grew,” he said. “Louder. Tighter. It did not flow with the long songs of the deep. It… pulled. It turned… this way.” One of the patterns along his side shifted, showing spirals flattening into forced arcs. “Currents that should curve… were straightened. Paths that should wander… were bound. The little ones… lost their ways.”

Mara imagined shoals of fish suddenly finding their migration routes blocked or twisted. Eggs laid in the wrong places. Feeding grounds missed. Whales arriving where krill no longer gathered.

“Some of us,” the Whale went on, “pushed against it. We sang twice, thrice, louder. We made our own… hum. Madam felt it. She is not… gentle when irritated.”

His lanterns flickered, a faint, rueful pattern that the translation net rendered as a puff of wryness.

“She pushed… back,” he said. “She took the hum-maker’s song”—a tilt of his massive head toward Professor Finn—“and shook it. Doors that were shut… rattled. Cracks… remembered they were cracks. The long sleep of some things… creased.”

Mara thought of the tidepool door, of the way the engine in Finn’s workshop had started to leak little images of the surface. Of the hairline crack that had appeared near the top of the Veil graph no one had noticed.

“Is she… angry with Pelagoria?” Mara asked, before she could stop herself.

The Whale’s eye glimmered.

“Madam does not… anger as you do,” the voice said. “She does not wish ill. She wishes… balance. She wishes… movement. Your city asked to be still. To hide. To bind its edges so tight that nothing could get in. Or out.”

His lanterns flared, then dimmed, in a pattern Mara felt more than understood: the sense of something squeezed too hard.

“The Veil… is fear, made into current,” he said. “Fear of hooks. Of poison. Of… forgetting. It hums always now. It hums so loud that the old songs… fray.”

“The old songs?” Liora echoed, frowning.

“The songs of tides,” the Whale said. “Of seasons under water. Of where the warm streams run and the cold rise. Of when the little fish become big. Of when the storms roll long. We sang them together, long ago. The great ones, the small, Madam, the Stone. You—we—” A complicated wash of tones that the net struggled to translate. “—your people—used to add your own noise. Boats. Nets. Laughing at the sky. We… listened. We moved. We changed. Then you… stopped.”

“We hid,” Liora said softly.

“Yes,” the Whale said. “You wrapped yourselves in a humming cloak and said, ‘We will not be seen. We will not be touched. We will not be… hurt.’ But the hum does not care why it sounds. It only cares that it is loud.”

He shifted again. As his vast tail unfurled briefly in the gloom, Mara’s stomach swooped; the sense of scale made the bubble feel suddenly very, very small.

“The little ones…” His lanterns along his flank flickered weakly in one section, their glow uneven. “Some do not find the places they remember. Some… do not remember. The great ones…” A deeper pulse rolled through the water. “We feel… wrong. Like swimming with half a song. Like turning in a current that has forgotten how to turn.”

Mara’s chest hurt. “You’re… in pain,” she said.

“There is… ache,” the Whale agreed, in the careful way of someone who does not like to use the words hurt and I in the same sentence. “But more than that, there is… thinness. The world is… thinner where the hum rubs. Threads wear. Doors… remember. Things below… stir.”

He let that last word hang.

Liora’s fingers tightened where they gripped the inner surface of the bubble. “And the Door that Mara fell through?” she asked. “That was… the sea’s doing?”

“Madam’s,” the Whale said. “And yours.”

“Ours?” Mara said. “We didn’t… I mean, I did follow it, but—”

“You hummed,” the Whale said. “You, bright-eyes. Question-heart. When you put your hand on the wall of the half-city on the shore. When you touched the old helmet. When you stepped into the Door in the small-pool. You hummed with us. You did not know. Your hum is… young. But it is… loud in its way.”

Mara remembered the museum’s pulsing wall under her palm. The drip from the helmet. The way the tidepool had seized her hand and gently, inexorably, pulled.

“So I… made it worse,” she said, with a horrible little twist in her stomach. “I… made the cracks… crackier.”

“You made them… seen,” the Whale corrected. “Fear binds. Curiosity… loosens. Both are needed. If only one sings, the sea… limps.”

Liora’s face was pale, even under the soft blue light of the lanterns.

“We did this,” she whispered. “Our Veil. Our… fear. To stay safe from the Surface, we hurt… all of this.”

She gestured, helplessly, at the surrounding dark: at the faint glows of distant creatures, the slow drift of particles, the invisible but ever-present movement of water itself.

“We asked to be hidden so hard that the sea bent,” she said. “And now… everyone else pays for it.”

Mara thought of fences on the surface. Of neighbours putting up higher and higher hedges to avoid having to talk to each other over the back fence. Of borders drawn on maps that sliced through rivers and migration routes and people’s lives because someone far away had been frightened of mixing.

She thought of her own bedroom door, shut tight when she was angry or scared, the way the air in her room had grown stale and thin when she stayed inside too long.

“My people… build walls,” she said, quietly. “Sometimes for good reasons. To keep out… war, or storms, or bad ideas. Sometimes just because they’re scared. They always say it’s safer. But the walls… do things. They change… everything around them. People can’t get to food. Or to each other. Or to… safety. And sometimes the people who built the walls end up trapped behind them.”

She swallowed. The words felt heavy. No adult back home had wanted to hear this kind of thing from a ten-year-old. They’d said things like, It’s complicated, dear. As if complication canceled consequence.

“We thought we were protecting ourselves,” Liora said. “From hooks. From nets. From oil. From… surface greed. And we did. But we hurt you. And all the others.”

The Whale’s lanterns shimmered.

“Fear is a wave,” he said. “It rolls. It hits one shore. It rebounds. It hits another. If you build a wall, the wave does not stop. It breaks… differently.”

Mara pictured it: a high concrete barrier on a beach, waves slamming against it, water spraying up and sideways, eating away at other parts of the shore.

“You must… soften the hum,” the Whale said. “Loosen the cloak. Let some songs through. Or Madam will tear it for you. And when she tears, she does not… mend with care.”

Mara shivered.

“How?” Liora asked, voice rough. “If we loosen the Veil, the Surface will see us. They’ll send ships. And nets. And… drills. And garbage.”

“Some will,” the Whale said. “Some will not. They are… many. They do not hum all the same. You cannot… hide forever. Even at the bottom, we hear their… thunder. Feel their… heat. They melt the ice-songs. They thicken the currents with… trash. Your cloak hums… louder with fear. Their boats… scrape louder with greed. The songs… clash. The sea cannot… carry both without… splitting.”

Finn winced. “That’s… roughly what the graphs say,” he admitted. “In fewer metaphors and more terrifying numbers.”

Mara pressed her forehead against the inner wall of the bubble, closing her eyes for a moment.

She saw, in the dark behind her eyelids, the lines on Finn’s paneled graph wobbling, intersecting, spiking. The smeared whale-song marks. The red door-spikes. The dotted threshold.

“How bad is it?” she asked. “The… tearing.”

The Whale was quiet for a long time.

“There are… places,” he said at last, voice lower. “Where warm meets cold… too sharply. Where the currents… whirl without… reason. Little fish gather… and find nothing. Great ones follow… paths that end in… heat. The ice-songs… at the top grow thin. The deep… grows hungrier.”

He shifted, and for a moment their bubble drifted over the edge of something.

Mara’s stomach swooped again. She opened her eyes.

The seafloor, which had been a reasonably comforting, if distant, presence beneath them, fell away.

The trench yawned.

It was not a neat valley. It was a rent. The ground sheared downward in jagged steps and broken shelves, vanishing into black that swallowed even the Whale’s lantern-glow. Faint flashes of light winked in that depth—isolated, stubborn, like stars glimpsed through storm-cloud.

The edges of the trench were not smooth. In some places, rock had slumped, forming landslides of rubble that poured partially into the darkness. In others, walls jutted out in sharp, layered cliffs. Here and there, Mara saw cracks in the rock, thin lines of faint, sickly green glow seeping out, like bruises.

A low, constant crackle reached them, even through the insulating skin of the bubble. It sounded like glass being very slowly, very insistently strained.

“The Stone… complains,” the Whale said. “Pressure… moves. Heat… seeps. Old… things… stir.”

“Please stop saying that,” Bubbles whimpered quietly.

Finn adjusted a lever with shaking fingers. “We’re not going any closer to the bottom,” he said firmly. “Queen’s orders. My orders. The Stone’s strong suggestion.”

Mara could feel it, though: an enormous presence below. Not the Whale. Something… broader. Slower. Like an old thought at the back of the sea’s mind, turning.

Her skin crawled. Every instinct screamed that she was somewhere she was not meant to be, hanging in a fragile sphere above a hunger too big for the word.

“Do not wake,” the Whale said, and for the first time there was something like fear, clean and sharp, in his ancient voice. “Do not call. Do not… name.”

“We won’t,” Liora said, hoarse. “I promise. We came to listen. Not to shout.”

The Whale’s lanterns along his flank softened.

“You listen,” he acknowledged. “You ask. That is… new. Once, your people shouted from ships and… did not hear. Now you hide and… do not see. Both are… dangerous.”

He tilted his vast head slightly, regarding their tiny bubble. “Half-child,” he said to Liora. “Glass-Tide. You walk in two hums. You can… carry songs between. Sky-breath,” he said to Mara. “Door-fallen. You touch walls and they… answer. You can… hear cracks.”

Mara’s hand tightened involuntarily around the strap of her backpack. Somewhere up there, in a museum held together by dust and narrative momentum, there was a wall that had pulsed under her palm.

“Maker of humming,” the Whale said to Finn. “You tied your clever machine to Madam’s skirts. You hear her… tug. You must… loosen your knots. Or they will… snap.”

Finn swallowed, Adam’s apple bobbing under his beard. “I built the Engine to protect us,” he said, almost to himself. “To keep ships from finding us. To keep… another Thimble from… stumbling in. I didn’t… mean…”

“Intent is a small fish,” the Whale said. “It can be eaten by… larger ones.”

Mara winced. The image lodged itself too neatly in her imagination.

“So we… change it,” Liora said, fierce now. “We… tune it differently. Softer. So it hums with your songs, not over them.”

“You talk to your Queen,” the Whale said. “Tell her… the cloak is too tight. Tell her… hiding has a cost. She knows. She does not like to… know. You hum in her ears.”

“I’m good at humming in people’s ears,” Liora muttered.

“And you,” the Whale said to Mara. “You go… up. Watch the sky-water. Listen to the storms. Watch where the shore… moves. Tell the hum-maker… and the Queen-child. Tell the half-city man with the wooden leg.”

“Captain Thimble,” Mara said.

“Yes,” the Whale said. “He hums… guilt. It is… heavy. He may… help, if he… sings differently.”

Mara thought of Thimble’s face when he’d seen the dripping helmet in the museum. The locked, guilty muscles around his eyes. The way he’d pulled her hand from the wall.

“We’ll… tell him,” she said. “If I can get back. If the Door—”

“The Door… remembers you now,” the Whale said. “It will not… forget quickly. Madam likes… pattern. You are… a pattern.”

Mara wasn’t entirely sure she liked the sound of that, but the alternative—never reaching her shore again, never warning anyone up there—was worse.

“We’ll… try,” she said. “To fix it. To… loosen the cloak. Without…” She glanced down at the trench, where the darkness felt like a held breath. “…without tearing it.”

The Whale’s eye watched her for a long, long moment.

“Small promises are better than… large lies,” he said. “You are… small. Your promise… fits.”

Mara couldn’t tell if that was comforting or not.

“Time,” Bubbles whispered urgently. “Please say we have had enough of this. The pressure is… a lot of pressure.”

Finn checked a gauge, then another. His brow furrowed.

“He’s right,” he said reluctantly. “We can’t stay at this depth much longer. The hull’s fine, but the air mix… isn’t happy. And I’d rather not find out what happens to the translation net if we all pass out.”

“Then go,” the Whale said. “Up… and out. Do not… be slow. The Stone… shifts.”

As if in agreement, a faint tremor ran through the water. Far below, in the trench’s throat, a small cascade of gravel avalanched off a ledge and disappeared into the black.

“Right,” Finn said, flipping a series of switches with fingers that shook only a little. “Retract fins. Trim ballast. Bubbles, up, please.”

“With… absolute… pleasure,” Bubbles said fervently.

The fan-fins flexed and angled. The bubble-boat shuddered, then began to rise.

The Lantern Whale drifted alongside for a few heartbeats, his lanterns pulsing slowly. For a moment, their tiny sphere and his vast bulk were level, eye to eye.

“Remember,” he said, voice following them up through the dark. “Fear that forgets… to listen… is as dangerous as… greed that forgets… to share. The sea holds… both. You must… teach it… a new… song.”

Then he rolled, huge body corkscrewing with lazy grace, and glided off along the edge of the trench, lanterns marking his path like a string of floating moons.

The bubble-boat climbed.

The darkness thinned by imperceptible degrees. Faint hints of blue seeped back into the black. The crackling of strained rock faded, replaced by the more familiar susurrus of open water.

Mara realised she’d been clenching every muscle in her legs. She forced them to unclench, one by one, feeling as shaky as if she’d run a race.

Her head buzzed—not just from the odd air, but from the weight of what they’d heard.

We did this. We hurt him. We hurt all of them.

Liora stared up at the dimming glow of the Whale’s lanterns, her jaw tight.

“I thought hiding was the only way to keep us safe,” she said quietly. “Us, Pelagoria. From your kind.”

Mara flinched, but Liora shook her head, eyes still on the water above.

“I still… don’t trust the Surface,” she said. “Not all of it. Not the big ships and the people who dump things in rivers and the ones who think the sea is a bin.” A wry, bitter twist pulled at her mouth. “But… I didn’t think about… everyone else. The fish. The… kelp. The… Stone.”

“The Whale,” Mara said.

Liora’s voice cracked, just for a syllable. “Him,” she said. “We tied ourselves in a knot and choked his song.”

Mara thought of the fences around fancy houses on the cliffs above her town, shutting out wind and view, throwing their own small climates onto the beach below. The way her school’s rules sometimes hurt the kids they were meant to protect more than the ones they were protecting them from.

“We do that too,” she admitted. “On land. We build things to keep ‘bad stuff’ out and then pretend there’s no cost.” She chewed her lip. “And people who say, ‘Maybe this isn’t great’ get called… dramatic. Or told they don’t understand. Because they’re kids. Or because it’s ‘complicated.’”

Liora huffed. “They say that here, too. ‘Complicated.’ ‘Ancient agreements.’ ‘You’re too young.’” She flicked a tear angrily from the corner of her eye. In the thin air, it glistened for a second on her scale-dusted cheek before dripping onto her tunic. “Well. The sea didn’t think we were too young to listen.”

Mara swallowed. Her chest felt too full.

“Maybe… that’s the point,” she said. “We’re not… carrying all the old… fear yet. Or the old… excuses.”

Liora glanced at her, a quick, sharp look that said, without words, I am both terrified and glad you’re here.

“Professor?” Mara said, turning to Finn. His hands moved automatically on the controls, but his eyes were far away, replaying graphs and whale-song and old decisions.

“Yes?” he said absently.

“You built the Engine,” she said. “To protect Pelagoria. You didn’t… mean to…” She gestured downward, taking in trenches and currents and lantern-scars. “But you did. What are you going to do?”

Finn’s beard fluttered as he blew out a long breath.

“I am going to apologise,” he said. “First to the Queen. Then, if I can bear it, to the Council. Then, perhaps, to the Whale, though I suspect he already knows I am sorry.” He adjusted a valve with more tenderness than before. “And then I am going to change my machine. Not because all fear is foolish. Some of it keeps us from putting our hands in fires. But because I have been… listening only to one fear. The fear of being seen. I have not listened enough to the fear of… breaking what we sit in.”

He looked at Mara. Really looked, seeing not just the land-child who had blundered into his city but someone the Whale had named.

“And I am going to ask for help,” he added. “From a half-child and from a door-fallen bright one. Because clearly my generation’s very careful, very reasonable solutions have… problems.”

The bubble-boat shuddered softly as it passed through another thermocline. Bubbles of some gas fizzed and popped against its skin, tiny fingers tapping.

Ahead, high above, a faint glow strengthened: Pelagoria’s lights, smudged but real, like a cluster of candles in a far window.

“I don’t know how to tell my mother,” Liora said abruptly. “That her cloak is strangling her Whale. And her sea. And… maybe her chances of ever trusting the Surface again.”

“Carefully,” Finn suggested.

“Loudly,” Mara said.

Liora snorted, half-laugh, half-sob. “Both,” she decided. “Definitely both.”

Outside, as they rose, shadowy shapes peeled away from the trench’s edge, dispersing into the higher water. A school of small, bright fish spun around the bubble-boat in a quick, curious spiral before zipping off to some appointment with a safer current.

Far below, the trench yawned, unbothered by three small hearts beating hard in a fragile sphere.

The sea held its secrets. But now, at least, it had shared one of them.

As Pelagoria’s halo swelled ahead, Mara pressed her hand once more to the inside of the bubble. She thought of the museum wall under her palm, of the Whale’s old, vast voice in her bones, of Nerissa’s tight jaw when she said the word safe.

“We broke your song,” she thought downward, toward the fading lantern-glow. “We’re going to try to fix it. Even if it scares us.”

The water around them hummed, just a fraction, as if in answer.

Whether that was Madam Ocean’s approval, the Engine’s malfunction, or her imagination, Mara couldn’t say.

But as the bubble-boat paddled up toward the city of glass and currents, carrying with it the weight of what they’d seen—a wounded Whale, a strained Stone, a shield that strangled the very sea it asked for shelter—she knew one thing for certain:

Doors, once opened, did not like to close again. Not properly. Not if they’d seen there were other ways to be.

And somewhere above, on a storm-prone shore, a shabby museum waited, its walls full of old hums and its keeper full of old guilt, both of them about to find out that hiding from the deep was never as simple as it looked on a map.
