# Liora and the Library of Whispers

Where Mara meets Liora, the mer-child who has been secretly studying surface legends in a hidden library and decides Mara is the key to an ancient prophecy.

Pelagoria’s throne hall looked as if someone had tried to build a cathedral and an aquarium in the same spot, lost patience with both, and then decided to let the sea finish the job.

Mara sat on a smooth, pale bench that had grown out of the floor in a vague attempt at being a chair. It was too high for her feet to reach the ground properly, so her heels hovered, making her feel even more like a small child in a very large, very important waiting room.

The chamber was vast, oval, its high ceiling lost in dim, wavering light. The walls were not walls in the strict, comforting sense. They were thick, curved glass or something like it, banded with pale ribs of calcified coral. Beyond them, the ocean pressed close—an endless, rippling, blue-green presence just beyond the transparent shell. Fish the size of her hand darted past in bright flickers. Shadows of larger shapes drifted slowly in the middle distance, like thoughts too big to surface.

The floor was a pale, fused-sand mosaic in swirling patterns, the lines echoing waves and currents. At the far end of the hall, a raised platform curved gently up from the floor, supporting a broad, low seat that was more grown than carved. It was made of coral in shades from cream to deepest red, polished smooth where a person might sit but still bristling with delicate, living outgrowths elsewhere, tiny polyps questing at the water-scented air.

Queen Nerissa had taken that seat when they’d brought Mara in, her coral crown catching the light. She’d asked more questions, measured and precise, each one sinking hooks into Mara’s story: the tidepool, the shells, the museum-that-wasn’t, the man called Thimble Kelp. The name had made Nerissa’s nostrils flare, just slightly.

“You will remain here,” the Queen had said at last, her gaze like a weight. “Until I decide your place in this.”

This had not been encouraging.

Then she and her retinue had swept out through an arched doorway behind the coral throne, leaving Mara with two guards at the distant entrance, a lingering sense of having accidentally become a state secret, and a room full of carved coral guardians.

They lined the sides of the hall, ten on each curve: figures half again as tall as Mara, grown from stone and coral and some glassy substance that caught the light inside itself. Their faces were stylised—eyes large and slightly slanted, mouths composed, hair fanning back like waves. Their arms folded across their chests, each held a different object: a trident, a scroll, a shell, a coiled rope. Fins and scaled patterns edged their limbs.

At first, Mara had thought they were just elaborate decorations. Then she’d noticed the eyes.

They were not painted. They were not carved. They were little ovals of some dark, translucent stone—or maybe something alive, trapped or resting—and they caught every shift in the light. When she shifted on her bench, she was almost sure that a couple of those glossy pupils tracked the movement.

“Hello,” she told the nearest statue under her breath, because politeness cost nothing. “If you’re going to come to life dramatically, can you at least warn me first? Maybe do a little stretch? Rattle your coral? No? All right then.”

The statue sent her the blank, patient stare of things that had been asked much better questions.

The palace hummed around her.

It wasn’t a sound, exactly, more a layered thrum that she felt in her ribs rather than her ears. The faint, constant vibration of pumps and flows, the quieter murmur of many voices beyond the closed doors, all braided together. The air tasted faintly charged, like the moment before thunder when the sky holds its breath.

Occasionally, faint, warped snippets of conversation reached her through the heavy doors at the far end, carried on little eddies of air. The translation shell Nerissa had snapped into being still fluttered in the space around her head and ears, turning the burbled Pelagorian syllables into meaning.

“…fell straight through…”

“…Queen’s Door…”

“…hasn’t opened since—”

“…Professor Finn will—”

The last was cut off by a sound that was either a choked laugh or a groan.

Mara swung her feet, heels thudding softly against the bench’s side. Her brain, ever helpful, began producing worst-case scenarios in a neat, escalating list. She was going to be locked in a tower. Or sent back through the door in pieces. Or politely erased. Or—

The wall to her left sighed.

It was a soft, almost embarrassed sound: stone shifting against stone, coral flexing. Mara froze, halfway through inventing a fate involving being fed to something with too many teeth.

A narrow section of wall—between two of the carved guardians, where a vertical pattern of shell inlay had looked purely decorative—slid sideways. It moved silently once the initial shudder had passed, like a well-oiled secret.

Behind it, a low, dark gap yawned.

Out of that slot in the wall, as if she had just swum through an invisible skin of water and popped into air, stepped a girl.

She was dripping, but in a considered sort of way, like a painting of someone mid-surfacing. Droplets of water clung to her skin and clothes, running in tiny rivulets to pool at her bare feet. Her hair—long, dark, and thick—floated around her head in a slow, untidy halo, only half-subject to gravity. When she moved, it lagged a split-second behind, like it was deciding whether to come along.

Her tunic reached mid-thigh, woven from some pale, supple material that clung when wet. It was edged with faintly iridescent bands where scales showed along her shoulders and arms. Delicate fins fanned from the outer edges of her forearms like translucent sleeves tucked back. A faint spray of tiny, opalescent scales dusted her cheeks and temples, catching the light when she tilted her head.

Her eyes were the first thing Mara really processed: they were a deep, human brown, not the pale, uncanny colours of some of the guards. They were also bright with a particular brand of mischief that Mara recognised from every time she had been told not to touch something and then absolutely, definitely touched it.

The girl slipped fully into the hall, reached back, and eased the panel closed with practised fingers. The seam vanished, leaving only the unbroken pattern of shell inlay and stone.

Then she turned to Mara and smiled like they were old co-conspirators.

“There you are,” she said briskly. “Took you long enough.”

Mara stared. “I—what?”

The girl snorted, planting her hands on her hips. “Honestly, surface-doors. Always so… theatrical. Falling, glowing, doom-doom-doom.” She rolled her eyes. “The stories never mention how long it takes.”

“I only found it this morning,” Mara protested, more on reflex than anything. “It’s not like—wait. How did you—who are you?”

“Oh.” The girl straightened, remembering, perhaps, that other people might require introductions. “Liora of the Glass Tides. Princess of Pelagoria.” She pulled a face. “Unfortunately.”

She flicked her fingers, as if scattering the title away like stray droplets.

Mara’s brain, which had only just stopped shrieking about queens and bubbles, added princess to its crowded, blinking noticeboard. “I—um. Mara Bright. Of… the upstairs bit.”

“Of the Surface,” Liora translated with faint disdain, as if “upstairs bit” were a far superior term. “I know. You’re dripping land all over the place.”

“I am not dripping—” Mara started, then looked down.

She was, in fact, leaving small damp prints on the pale bench, and her shoes had collected fine grains of sand from the tidepool rim. A tiny pebble chose that moment to fall from the tread of one sole and ping gently off the floor.

Liora’s gaze followed it, then flicked back up to Mara’s face.

“See?” she said. “Surface. You all come down here shedding geography.”

Mara opened her mouth, uncertain whether to apologise on behalf of her species. “You said you were expecting me?”

“Not you, specifically,” Liora said. “But someone like you. A child from above. Door-fallen. Legend-flavoured.” Her eyes shone as she said it, as if the words themselves tasted good. “I’ve been watching the Door for years. Just in case.”

“Everyone keeps talking about doors,” Mara said. “Capital D doors. As if there’s… more than one?”

“There were,” Liora said. “Once. Now there’s this one. And it wasn’t supposed to open again. At least, that’s what my mother says. Which,” she added, with the swift, sideways glance of someone very used to situational disobedience, “means it definitely would.”

She glanced toward the big, official doors at the end of the hall, where the guards stood in their rigid positions, staring resolutely forward and pretending not to listen.

“You’ve met my mother?” Liora asked. “Tall, regal, draped in coral, looks like she’d apologise to you for drowning you?”

“Queen Nerissa,” Mara said. “Yes. She… asked a lot of questions. And then told me to sit.”

“That sounds like her.” Liora’s mouth crooked. “Everyone’s very agitated about you, by the way. It’s great. They keep trying to whisper, but the sound carries.” She wrinkled her nose. “And they won’t let me in on any of the good bits. ‘Not for the young, Liora’.” She imitated Nerissa’s cool, measured tone with unnerving accuracy. “‘Not your concern, Liora.’ ‘We mustn’t frighten you with politics, Liora.’ As if I’m not already frightened by having to attend Current Etiquette lessons twice a week.”

Mara blinked. “Current… etiquette?”

“Oh yes,” Liora said dryly. “How to bow at the correct angle to a delegation from the Eastward Gyres. Which side of your head to wear your coral when there’s a tsunami season. What to do if a current refuses to stop at a border. Very useful. Practically screaming with relevance.” She rolled her eyes toward the high, curving ceiling. “Whereas ancient prophecies involving mysterious children from beyond the sky apparently are not my concern.”

“Ancient prophecies,” Mara repeated weakly. “Involving… me.”

Liora’s gaze sharpened. “You came through the Queen’s Door,” she said. “Without a suit. Without a breath-stone. You shouldn’t have survived the Tunnel. And yet here you are, sitting on one of our least comfortable benches.” She shrugged. “So yes. Involving you.”

Mara felt a prickle run down her spine. “I didn’t ask to be a prophecy.”

“No one does,” Liora said matter-of-factly. “If they did, there would be forms and committees. And even more etiquette. Come on.”

She moved toward Mara with a quick, efficient stride that made the water still slick on her skin spatter delicately across the floor. Up close, Mara could see that Liora was about her own age, give or take a year. There was a faint crease between her brows that suggested she frowned at books a lot, and at adults even more.

“Come on what?” Mara said, as Liora reached out and caught her wrist, tugging.

“Before someone thinks of a Very Responsible Thing to do with you,” Liora said. “Like putting you in a room with no exits but teaching tapestries. Or handing you over to Professor Finn without supervision. You’ll drown in enthusiasm.” She shuddered theatrically. “No, thank you. I’ve waited too long for one of you to drop in just to let you be taken over by old people.”

Mara dug her heels in, more out of habit than conviction. “The guards will notice,” she hissed, glancing toward the far end of the hall.

Liora didn’t even look. “No, they won’t,” she said. “The panel’s shielded, and they’ve got their backs turned.” She flashed Mara a quick, wicked grin. “Trust me. I’ve been sneaking out of this hall since before I could swim a straight line.”

Mara had time to think, You probably still can’t swim a straight line, you’re too busy zigzagging, before a different, heavier thought dropped in behind her ribs.

If she stayed, she would sit and wait, while decisions were made in rooms where she was not allowed, about things that affected whether she went home or never saw the surface again. If she went with this strange, dripping princess, she might at least get some information—and possibly, if she was very lucky, some say in her own story.

“I am absolutely going to regret this,” she muttered, to herself more than anyone.

“Good,” Liora said cheerfully. “Regret means you picked the interesting path. Come on.”

She yanked lightly on Mara’s wrist. Mara slid off the bench, nearly catching her foot on a decorative swirl in the floor, and followed.

The carved coral guardians watched them go with unreadable, glossy eyes.

Liora pressed her palm against the shell-inlaid section of the wall again. Tiny, iridescent motes of light stirred under the surface at her touch, like schools of microscopic fish. The panel sighed open, the faint sound swallowed quickly by the hum of the hall.

“After you,” Liora said.

“But you—” Mara began. “You came out through there. Don’t you know—?”

“Of course I know. But you’re the one who fell through a door,” Liora pointed out. “You clearly have experience with dangerous thresholds. Consider this a practical exam.”

Mara shot her a look that said clearly We are going to talk about your sense of humour later, then ducked into the narrow opening.

The air inside was cooler, tinged with a stronger scent of minerals and the faint, metallic bite of worked shell. The passage—the word corridor felt too generous—was only just wide enough for Mara’s shoulders, its walls curving slightly inward, forcing her to hunch. A faint luminosity came from somewhere in the stone itself, the glow of creatures or crystals embedded within.

Behind her, the panel slid shut with a soft click. The throne hall and its watchful guardians were cut off as cleanly as if Mara had stepped out of a page.

For a heartbeat, the closeness of the space pressed in. Then Liora wriggled past her with the eel-like ease of someone used to small spaces and inconvenient physics.

“This way,” she said. “Mind your head. The palace was designed by people who thought children should fall in line, not run around in the walls.”

“That sounds familiar,” Mara muttered, ducking as the tunnel jogged sharply upward and then to the right.

The next few minutes were a blur of motion and stone.

The secret passages of Pelagoria were not, Mara discovered, a tidy network. They were more like the root system of an old tree gone half-feral: twisting, overlapping, occasionally doubling back on themselves as if they’d changed their mind mid-construction.

Sometimes the floor sloped gently; sometimes they had to scramble up what was essentially a set of rough-hewn steps, catching at small protrusions for balance. Twice, Liora took a sharp left that looked, to Mara’s eyes, exactly like a cliff face, and revealed at the last second that there was, in fact, a narrow, sideways slot that opened if you knew exactly which barnacle to press.

“Who built these?” Mara panted, after squeezing through a particularly tight kink that deposited her in a short, vertical shaft like the inside of a chimney.

“Depends who you ask,” Liora said, already halfway up, her fingers and toes finding holds that Mara’s eyes could barely register. “Officially, they’re maintenance channels. Unofficially, they’re the reason I still live. You can’t grow up in a palace and not develop emergency escape routes.”

“I meant—” Mara hauled herself up, her backpack catching on a projecting bit of shell. Liora reached down and gave it a corrective tug. “I meant… architecturally. Did you… grow them? Or… carve them?”

“A bit of both,” Liora said. “The first ones were fused from sand and shell with old heatstones. Then coral moved in. Then people moved in. Then I moved in.” She flashed a quick grin over her shoulder. “And any passage a child uses stops belonging to adults.”

They twisted around another corner. A faint murmur of voices filtered through the stone on one side, as if a group of people were holding a meeting only a few handspans away. The translation shell picked up fragments.

“…seal the Gate…”

“…the Lantern Whale’s last pass was late…”

“…you’d have us trust a surfacer—?”

Liora pressed a finger to her lips, eyes suddenly serious. She steered Mara away, down a steeper incline. The voices faded.

“Where are we going?” Mara whispered, partly because loudness felt disrespectful and partly because her lungs were starting to realise that Pelagorian air lacked some of the surface’s comforting density.

“Library,” Liora whispered back. “Mine. Mostly. The one no one wants to admit is there.”

“You have a secret library?” Mara said, unable to keep the envy out of her voice. “That’s not fair. I barely have a secret drawer.”

“You can visit anyway,” Liora said. “You’ve done the hard part. You fell out of the sky.”

They emerged at last into a space that felt at once larger and dimmer than anywhere they’d been since leaving the hall.

The passage ended abruptly in a rough opening. Beyond it, the world expanded into a cavern.

Mara stepped through—and stopped dead.

The library of whispers was carved into the inside of a hollow cliff that jutted out from Pelagoria’s main cluster. The air bubble here was narrower, closer; the outer wall was a broad curve of transparent shell, through which the sea loomed dark and near. The rest of the cavern was shaped from stone and coral, smoothed and hollowed and lined.

Shelves grew from the rock.

They rose in irregular tiers along the walls, curving with the cave, some jutting out like ledges, others recessed niches. Instead of wood, they were made from braided, pale coral and bands of thick, translucent shell, their surfaces polished to a satin sheen by many hands. Bioluminescent algae had been coaxed to grow along their edges, glowing in slow pulses of soft blue and green, lighting the space with a perpetual undersea twilight.

The shelves were full.

Not of books, precisely, but of objects that wanted very badly to be read.

There were flat tablets of stone etched with tiny, careful spirals of script, their lines inlaid with some faintly glowing substance. There were sheets of something like transparent paper made from dried algae, stacked and curled, their surfaces painted with swirling symbols that pulsed with their own light. There were loops of shell threaded together, each bead carved with minute dots and lines.

Clusters of glassy globes—no, not glass; some thicker, organic material like the museum’s lanterns—sat in groups, each cradling a swirl of dim, moving light. Thin ribbons of silvery metal hung from pegs, chimes that made no sound but shimmered with text-like patterns when they moved.

At the centre of the cavern, a wide, shallow basin had been hollowed into the floor and filled with still water. Above it, suspended from the ceiling by delicate strands of braided weed, hung a spiral construct of shell and stone, shaped like a nautilus unfurling. Tiny lantern-creatures lived along its inner coils, shedding a gentle, pearly glow.

The whole space murmured.

The sound was faint but constant: a susurrus of movement, the soft tick of things settling, the almost-voices of stored words seeping into the air. Sometimes it rose, like a breath, then faded again, as if the library were dozing and talking in its sleep.

Liora inhaled deeply, her shoulders loosening.

“Home,” she said under her breath, with a satisfaction Mara recognised instantly: the feeling of stepping into a place that fit you like an idea you’d had alone and then found someone had built.

“You… made all this?” Mara asked, incredulous.

“Not all,” Liora said. “It was here before I was born. But most people pretend it isn’t. Officially, all knowledge is stored in the Hall of Records.” She pulled a face. “Which is across from the Hall of Tedious Things and next to the Chamber of Arguments. So I found this instead.”

“How?” Mara asked.

Liora shrugged, moving toward one of the lower shelves. Her bare feet made no sound on the smooth ground.

“I was supposed to have a lesson with the Tidal Historian,” she said. “She fell asleep in mid-lecture. I went exploring. There was a draft. And a sound, like whispers. And a crack in the stone that someone had patched badly. After that, it was just… persistence.”

She ran her fingers affectionately along the edge of a shelf as she passed, as if greeting an old friend. The algae there brightened briefly under her touch.

“Mother doesn’t like it,” Liora added. “She thinks too many words in one place make waves. The Council would close it if they could find it. So it doesn’t exist. Except when I’m in it. And now when you are.”

Mara turned slowly, taking it in. Her fingertips tingled. “It’s… beautiful.”

“It’s an accident,” Liora said. “And a secret. And too damp for proper scrolls.” Her mouth twitched. “Which makes it perfect.”

Something bubbled at the edge of Mara’s hearing. She turned toward a shelf where small, clear jars were arranged in careful rows, each sealed with a waxed pinch of something dark. Inside each jar, a faint blue shape hung, like a miniature storm cloud.

“What are those?” she asked.

“Songs,” Liora said. “Mostly whales’. Some storm-voices. A few currents. Professor Finn got very excited when he discovered you could persuade sound to live in crystal. Everyone else got very concerned when he realised that meant arguments could be replayed forever.”

Mara moved closer. The nearest jar pulsed faintly, as if in time with some distant, remembered beat.

“Can I…?” She reached out, then caught herself. “Or will it explode? Or attract sharks?”

Liora huffed, amused. “They’re safe. Mostly. The lids are waxed shut. You have to crack the seal to let them out.” She picked up one of the jars, its contents swirling just slightly in response to the motion, and held it up to the light. “This one’s a lullaby, I think. Or a warning. It can be both.”

She set it back reverently. “Later,” she said. “First, I need to show you something else.”

She darted to a narrower shelf tucked into a crook of rock. On it rested a flat, circular object about the size of a large dinner plate: a disc of some translucent, pearly material, thin as shell but shot through with faint, swirling lines of colour.

When Liora touched the edge, it brightened.

Streams of light bloomed across its surface, rising up like smoke and then resolving into shifting lines and whorls in three dimensions. They hovered above the disc, forming a delicate, glowing model of something that looked at first like a map and then, as Mara’s eyes adjusted, like something stranger.

It was, she realised with a small, involuntary sound, the sea.

Not all of it, of course, but a section: an undulating plane representing the ocean’s surface, with faint, shimmering hints of landmasses at the edges. Below that, layers upon layers of curling lines in different shades of blue and green and faint gold looped and twined: currents, eddies, flows. Some moved rapidly, their light darting like quickfish; others turned ponderously, great, slow spirals.

Dots of pale light marked certain points: here a cluster, there a solitary shimmer. Tiny symbols glowed near some of them. The whole thing drifted and shifted subtly, never quite staying the same, as if it were a live feed from the idea of water itself.

Mara leaned in, eyes wide. “Is that…”

“Currents,” Liora said softly. “The ones near us, anyway. The shallow ones, the deep ones. The ones that circle, the ones that cut across and sulk. Professor Finn made it with a thousand little sensing shells and a lot of shouting. I… borrowed it.”

“Borrowed,” Mara repeated.

“He wasn’t using it,” Liora said defensively. “He was busy trying to teach a coral nursery the alphabet. This is important.”

Her finger traced one of the glowing lines that curved near the edge of the disc. It brightened under her touch, revealing a small, stylised shell symbol at one point along its length.

“Here,” she said. “This is Pelagoria. Our currents, our protections. The old songs say that as long as the Veil holds, the city stays hidden. Tides bend around us. Nets tear. Sonars fail. Doors stay shut.”

She flicked her fingers and another symbol appeared—higher up, toward the line representing the surface: a soft, pulsing dot.

“And this,” she said, “was the Door.”

“The tidepool,” Mara murmured.

“The Queen’s Door,” Liora corrected. “The one only she is supposed to be able to open. The one that used to be used for… exchanges.” Her eyes slid to Mara. “In the older stories. Back when we traded more than fear with the Surface.”

Mara thought of Captain Thimble Kelp’s battered museum, its walls pulsing under her hand. Of the dripping helmet and the map that had refused to stay still.

“There’s… another side,” she said. “Up there. It’s… attached to a room in a building. A… museum. On land.”

Liora’s voice quickened. “Is there a bell?” she asked. “A hanging rope? A ring of silver? A song written in a script you can’t quite read?”

“Um,” Mara said. “There’s a sign that says PRESS IF YOU MUST, but I think that’s just a bell.”

Liora shut her eyes briefly, exasperated and delighted at once. “Of course it is. Surface people are so dramatic.”

She opened them again. “Anyway. The Veil. It’s… complicated.” She chewed her lip, searching for the right explanation. “It’s a weaving of currents and old promises and… other things. It bends the water around us, hides us. The Queen and the Council say it is strong as ever. That we are safe. But…”

She hesitated.

“But what?” Mara asked quietly.

Liora tapped the glowing model. A few of the lines flickered, their light dimming for a heartbeat before returning.

“But there are shivers,” she said. “Little changes. Strange lag-times in the Lantern Whale’s calls. Old routes not holding as steady as they used to.” She nodded toward the far wall, where the sea pressed close. “And the Door opened when it should not. To someone it should not. I think… something is wrong.”

“Has anything… gotten in?” Mara asked. “Besides me, I mean.”

Liora shook her head. “Not yet. And if the adults have noticed anything, they aren’t saying.” Bitterness crept into her voice. “They say it’s nothing. That I read too much. That I take legends as if they were tide charts. That I make patterns where there are none.”

“That’s a lot of that they say,” Mara said.

“Exactly,” Liora said, sharp and pleased. “So I started collecting the things they didn’t want me to learn. Surface stories, old songs, fragments of treaties.” She gestured around at the shelves. “This is what they call forbidden. So I thought, It must be important.”

Mara’s mouth curved without her permission. “We have that on land, too,” she said. “If someone tells you not to read something ‘until you’re older,’ it usually means it has the good bits.”

“The good bits here are usually terrifying,” Liora said. “But also useful.”

She moved to another section of shelving. Long, flat pieces of shell leaned there, each etched with flowing script and small, careful illustrations. Liora pulled one free and laid it on a low, curved table grown from a protrusion of rock.

The script along its surface glowed faintly, like embers banked under ash.

“The Tale of the Twice-Born City,” she said. “The one I’m not supposed to know exists. The one that says the first Pelagoria sank, and then rose again somewhere no one could find. The one that mentions, in a footnote, the Promise of the Twin Horizons.”

“The… what?” Mara asked.

“Here.” Liora traced a line of text, and the translation shell hummed, obligingly weaving meaning into Mara’s understanding.

…when the water-sister and the sky-sister turn their faces in fear, a child from above and a child from below shall stand together in the place-between, seeing both ways. They shall mark the cracks in the glass tide and name the currents that forget their paths. Only through their joined eyes shall the Veil be mended, or else it shall tear and drown both worlds.

Mara’s skin prickled.

“That sounds…” She groped for a word that wasn’t ‘ominous.’ “…serious.”

“It’s a bad translation,” Liora said dismissively. “Professor Finn’s fault. He has very messy handwriting.” But her fingers lingered on the carved symbols, tracing them with a familiarity that spoke of many rereadings.

She looked up at Mara, eyes suddenly very bright, very intent.

“You fell through the Door the old songs talk about,” she said. “You’re a child from above. I’m a child from below. Half-below, anyway.” She flicked one of the small fins at her wrist. “We’re both… in-between.”

“I’m not in-between,” Mara protested. “I’m very firmly land. I get nosebleeds if I think about math too hard, I can’t swim in a straight line, and my mother thinks jellyfish are a political statement.”

“And yet, here you are,” Liora said. “Breathing our thin air. Standing in a place that doesn’t officially exist. Understanding me because the old translation net decided you were worth its trouble. My mother’s Veil let you through. The sea didn’t crush you. The Door held. Things that don’t happen, happened. That means something.”

Mara rubbed her arms, trying to chase away the goosebumps. “Where I come from,” she said slowly, “adults don’t believe in prophecies. They believe in… timetables. And test scores. And… recycling.”

“Where I come from,” Liora said, “adults say they don’t believe in prophecies, and then quietly adjust their entire foreign policy around lines in a song. They believe in currents, and treaties, and never, ever saying aloud that they are afraid.” She snorted softly. “I believe in patterns. And in being early to unpleasant truths.”

She straightened, suddenly brisk again. “Look. Whether or not you’re some Twice-Born Destiny-Child of the Sky, the fact is: the Door opened and the Veil is… itchy. Something’s wrong with our protections. If it fails, ships could find us. Nets. Drills. Poisoned rain. And if it fails big enough, it might not stop at us.”

“The sea could… drown the land?” Mara asked, quietly.

“The sea doesn’t hate the land,” Liora said. “It doesn’t love it either. It just… is. But when boundaries go wrong, everyone gets wet.” She met Mara’s gaze. “Your people don’t exactly have a good record with care.”

Mara felt her face burn. She thought of plastic washing up on their beach, of oil slicks she’d seen on the news, of the way rubbish bins along the promenade sometimes overflowed after tourist days, spilling wrappers into the wind.

“We’re not all like that,” she said, a little too fast.

“I know,” Liora said. “I’ve… read things.” Her mouth twisted. “And heard things. Some of the old stories say there was a time we traded songs and stories and… objects. Little strange things. There’s a whole section over there—” She jerked her chin toward a shelf where odd, familiar silhouettes lurked. “—of surface-things my ancestors thought were important enough to keep.”

Mara squinted. There was, unmistakeably, a rusty bicycle bell, a hairbrush with most of its bristles missing, a cracked snow globe with a faded, drowned-looking model of a city inside, and—she almost laughed—a fork.

“You hoarded our cutlery,” she said.

“It’s very good for catching slippery things,” Liora said defensively. “And we don’t have your sort of cheese. Anyway. The point is: our worlds used to touch. Then they stopped. The Veil thickened. The Doors sealed. The stories became forbidden. And now…” She spread her hands. “Now you. Here.”

Mara’s thoughts skittered like minnows. “Why me?” she blurted. “Why not some brilliant scientist? Or a… diplomat? Or at least someone who doesn’t trip over her own feet in gym class?”

Liora considered this with the seriousness of someone evaluating a tide’s mood.

“Maybe because you’re small enough to slip through cracks,” she said. “And stubborn enough to try. Big people get stuck. And they bring too many… things with them.” She flapped her hands in a vague gesture that encompassed bureaucracy, heavy boots, and poor listening skills. “You followed shells into a pool that was obviously wrong. Most surfacers would have taken a picture, posted it on… Flicker Snap or whatever you call it, and gone home.”

“Flicker Snap?” Mara said, despite herself.

Liora waved a hand. “I’m still working on my surface vocabulary. The point is: you’re the sort of person who goes where she’s not supposed to. So am I. That makes us useful.”

Mara looked at the swirling map of currents, at the glowing threads that pulsed and bent. At the point marked Pelagoria. At the faint, pulsing dot of the Door above it. At the lines that had flickered when Liora touched them.

Outside, beyond the shell wall, something vast and slow drifted past: a dark shape with speckles of light along its side. One of the lights flared brighter, then dimmed, like an eye opening and closing.

“The Lantern Whale,” Liora said, noticing her gaze. There was awe and affection in her voice. “He circles us. Sings to the Veil. Keeps it humming. He’s been… off, lately.”

“Off?” Mara said.

“His songs are late, or early, or… pitched wrong.” Liora’s brows knit. “You can feel it if you’ve listened to them enough. Most people don’t. They just say, ‘Oh, the tides are strange this season, how curious,’ and go back to complaining about algae in their filter-shells.”

Mara thought of Captain Thimble calling the storm unseasonable. Of the way the museum floor had seemed to breathe.

“What do you want to do?” she asked. The question surprised her as it left her mouth, but it felt right.

Liora’s eyes widened slightly, as if no one her age ever asked her that.

“Find the cracks,” she said simply. “Before something else finds them for us.” She gestured to the currents map. “If the Veil is fraying, it’ll show up in the flows. Tiny anomalies. Echoes where there shouldn’t be echoes. Fails to ripple. But I can’t see the surface currents as well as I’d like. Our sensors don’t reach up there easily. That’s where you come in.”

Mara blinked. “I don’t exactly have a pocket full of oceanographic equipment,” she said. “I have a notebook, three pens, a smushed cereal bar, and a phone my mum keeps threatening to take away.”

“Can your phone hear the sea?” Liora asked, dead serious.

“Not… usually,” Mara said. “It mostly hears my mother.”

“Pity,” Liora muttered. “Still. You can see things we can’t. From your side of the Veil. Strange storms. Odd tides. Door-behaviour. People-behaviour.” Her lips curled. “Surfacers are very loud. Patterns show up in your shouting.”

“And you can see things down here,” Mara said slowly. “Currents. Songs. Cracks. Grumpy whales.”

“Exactly.” Liora’s hand dropped to the glowing map, fingers splayed near the symbol for Pelagoria. “If we put what we know together… maybe we can see the whole pattern. Before it breaks.”

Mara chewed her lower lip.

“I’m ten,” she said. “I’m not… I’m not anyone important. On the surface, I mean. I’m just… Mara. I get told off for doodling in margins and leaving sand in my shoes. I’m not… chosen.”

Liora regarded her, head tilted, hair drifting gently as if underwater still decided it had claims on her.

“Down here,” she said, “I’m a princess no one really wants to listen to about the things I care about. Up there, you’re a girl no one listens to about the things you see. But the Door opened for you, Mara Bright from Above. The Veil twitched. The Whale sings wrong. My mother is afraid and won’t say so. That’s enough ‘important’ for me.”

The library hummed softly around them, as if in agreement.

Mara thought of being ushered into quiet corners while adults talked about climate and currents and “big issues,” as if her ears didn’t work if the words were over a certain length. Of seeing rubbish in the shallows and being told recycling was “being handled.” Of the way grown-ups liked their problems big and far away, not here and now and dripping brine on their tidy floors.

She looked at Liora: at the determined set of her jaw, at the flicker of nerves deep in her eyes that matched Mara’s own. At the mixture of fear and excitement coiled in her shoulders like a spring.

“Okay,” Mara said.

The word shook a little as it came out, but it was solid in her chest.

“Okay?” Liora echoed.

“Okay,” Mara repeated, firmer. “I don’t know how to fix… ocean physics. Or magical protective veils. Or whatever we’re dealing with. But I can… look. And listen. And tell you what I see up there. And… try not to drown.”

Liora’s grin unfurled slowly, bright as a rising lantern.

“Deal,” she said. She spat lightly into her palm and held out her hand.

Mara blinked at it. “Is that… is that a thing? Down here?”

“It’s a thing everywhere,” Liora said confidently. “Sworn on salt. Very binding. Very old. Very sticky.”

Mara wiped her own palm hastily on her jeans—because some habits were universal—and spat on it as well. Their hands met with an affectionate, damp smack.

A faint tingle ran up Mara’s arm, like static or the aftertaste of a good idea. The algae on the nearest shelf brightened for a heartbeat, then settled.

“Liora of the Glass Tides,” Liora said solemnly. “Princess of Pelagoria. Rule-breaker. Reader. Annoyance to elders.”

“Mara Bright,” Mara said, matching her tone as best she could. “Of… the upstairs bit. Question-asker. Door-faller. Perpetual detention risk.”

They held each other’s gaze for a moment that stretched and twisted like light through water.

Then Liora let go, wiping her hand on her tunic with only a brief wince at the smear.

“Right,” she said briskly. “Now that we’ve been extremely ceremonial, we can get to the good part.”

“The good part?” Mara said, flexing her tingling fingers.

“Questions,” Liora said, with relish. “So many questions. For instance: Is rain really as loud as the stories say? Is the sky always blue? Why do you put sharp things on your feet to walk on hard things? And why do you all insist on sleeping out in the open where anything could just fall on you?”

Mara snorted. “Is rain loud. You live under the entire ocean and you think rain is loud?”

“It sounds sharp,” Liora said defensively. “The drops. And the roof. We don’t have roof. We have… up.”

Mara perched on the edge of the rock table, drawn forward. “Fine. But I get to ask things too. Like: how do you not get lost when everything is blue? And how do you keep your hair from turning into a nest? And what does whale song actually… feel like?”

“Deal,” Liora said again. “We trade. A question for a question. Stories for stories. Surface for sea.”

They bent over the glowing map, heads close together, their voices threading through the soft murmur of the library.

As they traced currents with ink-stained fingers and argued in whispers about whether clouds were just floating algae or if Pelagorian jelly was better than peanut butter, the weight of prophecy and veils and failing protections did not vanish. It hovered at the edges of their awareness, like the vast shapes in the water beyond the shell wall.

But between them, in the narrow space carved out of stone and forbidden words, something else began to grow: a small, sturdy bridge of jokes and shared bewilderment and the extraordinary relief of being taken seriously by someone your own age.

Two children, one from below, one from above, bent over a map that showed neither world completely. In the faint glow of coral and thefted starlight, with the echoes of whale-song waiting in jars and old promises etched in shell around them, they started, in their own clumsy, determined way, to see the parts of the ocean neither the sea nor the land could see alone.
